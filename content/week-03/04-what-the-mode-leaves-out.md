[lesson:3.1] chose to climb the settled log joint $\hat\ell$ rather than the log
evidence $\ln p(u; \omega)$, because the evidence needs an integral over the state.
This lesson measures what that choice costs. The answer is a bias in every learned
parameter, present even when the world is generated by exactly the model the animal
holds, and in one case a collapse.

## The gradient of the evidence

::: derivation The Fisher identity
Write $p(u; \omega) = \int_0^\infty p(d, u; \omega)\,\mathrm dd$. Differentiate under the
integral, then multiply and divide the integrand by $p(d, u; \omega)$:

$$
\frac{\partial \ln p(u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}
\;=\; \frac{1}{p(u; \omega)}\int_0^\infty \frac{\partial p(d, u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\,\mathrm dd
\;=\; \int_0^\infty \frac{p(d, u; \omega)}{p(u; \omega)}\,\frac{\partial \ln p(d, u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\,\mathrm dd .
$$

The ratio in front is the posterior $p(d \mid u; \omega)$, and $\ln p(d, u; \omega)$ is
the log joint $\ell$. So

$$
\frac{\partial \ln p(u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}
\;=\; \mathbb E_{d\mid u}\!\left[\frac{\partial \ell(d, u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\right] ,
$$ {#fisher}

the average over the posterior $p(d \mid u; \omega)$ at the current parameters.

The first step differentiates under the integral sign. For a fixed observation $u$
and parameters $(d_p, \theta, \Sigma_p, \Sigma_u)$ in a compact subset of
$\mathbb R \times (0, \infty)^3$, so that $d_p$ is bounded and $\theta$, $\Sigma_p$ and
$\Sigma_u$ are each bounded above and bounded away from zero, that is justified by one
integrable bound on $|\partial p(d, u;\omega)/\partial\omega|$.
The parameter derivatives multiply $p(d, u; \omega)$ by polynomials in $d$ and
$1/d$. Near $d = 0$ the likelihood contains $\exp\!\big(-(u - \theta/d^{2})^2/(2\Sigma_u)\big)$,
which falls like $\exp(-C/d^{4})$ for some $C > 0$ and so goes to zero faster than any
power of $1/d$ grows; at large $d$ the prior's Gaussian tail goes to zero faster than
any power of $d$ grows.
:::

The slope of the log evidence is the posterior average of the same partial derivative
the animal uses. The animal evaluates it at one point, the mode, where [eq:fisher]
averages it over the whole posterior.

At Week&nbsp;2's observation the posterior has mean ${{post_mean_w2:.4f}}$ and variance
${{post_var_w2:.4f}}$, against a mode of ${{mode_w2:.4f}}$. The two versions of each
derivative are:

| Parameter | At the mode | Posterior average | Slope of $\ln p(u)$, by finite difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| $d_p$ | ${{grad_dp_w2:.4f}}$ | ${{fisher_avg_dp:.4f}}$ | ${{fisher_num_dp:.4f}}$ |
| $\Sigma_p$ | ${{grad_vp_w2:.4f}}$ | ${{fisher_avg_vp:.4f}}$ | ${{fisher_num_vp:.4f}}$ |
| $\Sigma_u$ | ${{grad_vu_w2:.4f}}$ | ${{fisher_avg_vu:.4f}}$ | ${{fisher_num_vu:.4f}}$ |

The last two columns agree, as [eq:fisher] requires. The first does not: for $\Sigma_u$
it is several times too steep, and for $d_p$ it has the wrong sign. On this one sound the
evidence would be raised by moving the prior location up, and the animal moves it
down.

## Mean zero at the truth

One trial proves little. By [lesson:3.1], a rule is judged by where its drift
vanishes and whether that zero attracts.

::: definition Score
The **score** of a parameter is the slope of the log evidence in that parameter,
$\partial\ln p(u; \omega)/\partial\omega$, as a function of the observation $u$.
:::

::: derivation The score has mean zero
Suppose the world draws $u$ from $p(u; \omega^{*})$, the model's own evidence at some
true parameter $\omega^{*}$. The expected slope of the log evidence at the truth is

$$
\int p(u; \omega^{*})\,\frac{\partial \ln p(u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\bigg|_{\omega^{*}}\mathrm du
\;=\; \int \frac{\partial p(u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\bigg|_{\omega^{*}}\mathrm du
\;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial \omega}\int p(u; \omega)\,\mathrm du\,\bigg|_{\omega^{*}}
\;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\,1 \;=\; 0 .
$$

The second equality moves the derivative outside an integral over $u$. Unlike the
interchange in [eq:fisher], which has a fixed $u$, this one needs a bound that holds
over all observations as well, and the lesson assumes it rather than proving it. The
derivation of the drift below makes the same assumption.
:::

So the score has mean zero at the true parameter. Combined with [eq:fisher], it says that a rule
which averages $\partial\ell/\partial\omega$ over the posterior, rather than reading it
at the mode, has zero drift at the truth whenever the world is one of the model's own
settings.

::: definition Posterior-averaged rule and point rule
The **posterior-averaged rule** changes a parameter on trial $n$ by

$$
\omega \;\leftarrow\; \omega \;+\; \beta\,\mathbb E_{d\mid u_n}\!\left[\frac{\partial \ell(d, u_n; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\right] .
$$ {#post-rule}

The rule of [lesson:3.1] reads the same derivative at the mode
instead, and from here on is called the **point rule**.
:::

The animal does not have the posterior average; the simulations compute it on every
trial by quadrature over the posterior, on at least 801 points and more where the
result has not stopped changing, so that the two rules can be
compared on the same trials. Nothing guarantees zero drift at the truth for the point
rule.

::: derivation The posterior-averaged rule climbs towards the world
By [eq:fisher] the drift of [eq:post-rule] is the process average of the score,

$$
\varphi(\omega) \;=\; \mathbb E_{P^*}\!\left[\frac{\partial \ln p(u; \omega)}{\partial\omega}\right]
\;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\,\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\ln p(u; \omega)\big] .
$$

Write $P^*(u)$ for the density of the observations the process produces. Adding and
subtracting $\ln P^*(u)$ inside the average, as [lesson:1.2] did with surprise and
entropy, with the sum there replaced by an integral,

$$
\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\ln p(u;\omega)\big] \;=\; \mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\ln P^*(u)\big] \;-\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[P^*\,\|\,p(\cdot\,;\omega)\big] .
$$

The first term does not involve $\omega$, so

$$
\varphi(\omega) \;=\; -\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[P^*\,\|\,p(\cdot\,;\omega)\big] .
$$
:::

The drift is minus the slope, in $\omega$, of the divergence of the world's
distribution of sounds relative to the model's. When the world is one of the model's settings, that divergence
is zero at the truth and positive elsewhere, so the truth is a minimum of it: the drift
vanishes there, and where the divergence curves upwards, $\varphi' < 0$ and the truth
attracts. The score argument above is the first half of this; the second half is why
the posterior-averaged rule returns to the truth after a disturbance.

## The linear case

In a linear Gaussian model every expectation can be done by hand, so the size of the
bias can be computed exactly. Let the world draw $d$ from $\mathcal N(d_p, \Sigma_p^{*})$
on the whole line, and let $u$ be $d$ plus noise of variance $\Sigma_u$, so that $u$
has mean $d_p$ and variance $\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u$. The star marks the process, as
in [lesson:3.1]. The animal knows $d_p$ and $\Sigma_u$ and learns its prior variance
$\Sigma_p$, with no truncation.

::: derivation The stationary points of the point rule
With prior variance $\Sigma_p$, the mode is the precision-weighted average of
[lesson:2.4],

$$
\hat d - d_p \;=\; \frac{\Sigma_p}{\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u}\,(u - d_p) .
$$

The point rule's update for the prior variance is the derivative of [lesson:3.2]
without the truncation term, $\tfrac12\big((\hat d - d_p)^2/\Sigma_p^2 - 1/\Sigma_p\big)$.
Its average over the world uses $\mathbb E_{P^*}[(u - d_p)^2] = \Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u$:

$$
\mathbb E_{P^*}\!\left[\frac{(\hat d - d_p)^2}{\Sigma_p^2}\right] - \frac{1}{\Sigma_p}
\;=\; \frac{\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u}{(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2} - \frac{1}{\Sigma_p} .
$$

This vanishes where $\Sigma_p(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u) = (\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2$.
Expanded,

$$
\Sigma_p^2 + (\Sigma_u - \Sigma_p^{*})\,\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u^2 \;=\; 0 ,
\qquad
\Sigma_p \;=\; \tfrac12\Big[(\Sigma_p^{*} - \Sigma_u) \pm \sqrt{(\Sigma_p^{*} - \Sigma_u)^2 - 4\Sigma_u^2}\,\Big] .
$$ {#lin-roots}

Real positive roots need $\Sigma_p^{*} - \Sigma_u \geq 2\Sigma_u$, that is
$\Sigma_p^{*} \geq 3\Sigma_u$. Neither root is the truth unless $\Sigma_u = 0$:
substituting $\Sigma_p = \Sigma_p^{*}$ into the quadratic leaves
$\Sigma_p^{*}\Sigma_u + \Sigma_u^2$, which is positive.
:::

The sign of the drift settles which root attracts. Half the bracket above, put over
the common denominator $2\Sigma_p(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2$, is

$$
\varphi(\Sigma_p) \;=\; -\,\frac{\Sigma_p^2 + (\Sigma_u - \Sigma_p^{*})\,\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u^2}{2\Sigma_p(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2} .
$$

The denominator is positive, so the drift has the opposite sign to the quadratic in
[eq:lin-roots]. That quadratic is $\Sigma_u^2 > 0$ at $\Sigma_p = 0$ and positive for
large $\Sigma_p$, and its discriminant factorises as
$(\Sigma_p^{*} - \Sigma_u)^2 - 4\Sigma_u^2 = (\Sigma_p^{*} - 3\Sigma_u)(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u)$.
Three cases follow.

- $\Sigma_p^{*} < 3\Sigma_u$: no real root, the quadratic is positive throughout, the
  drift is negative for every $\Sigma_p$, and the learned variance falls towards zero.
- $\Sigma_p^{*} = 3\Sigma_u$: a double root at $\Sigma_p = \Sigma_u$, where the quadratic
  is $(\Sigma_p - \Sigma_u)^2$. The drift is negative on both sides and zero only there,
  so for the mean dynamics, which follow the drift, the root attracts from above and
  repels from below. A learner with a fixed rate does not follow the drift exactly. Its
  fluctuations can carry it below the root, and there the drift points down, but the
  observations are Gaussian and so unbounded, and one large enough observation gives
  an upward step that can take the variance back above the root. Whether the learner
  eventually collapses is a question about its sample paths that the drift alone does
  not answer.
- $\Sigma_p^{*} > 3\Sigma_u$: two roots. The drift is negative below the smaller,
  positive between them and negative above the larger. The smaller root repels and
  separates collapse from survival; the larger attracts, and is where the point rule
  settles.

Why the drift turns negative near zero can be read off the mode. The shrinkage factor
$\Sigma_p/(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$ in the mode depends on the variance being learned. A
smaller $\Sigma_p$ pulls each mode closer to $d_p$, so the modes' spread
$\Sigma_p^2(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u)/(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2$ shrinks like $\Sigma_p^2$
while the target it is compared with, $\Sigma_p$, shrinks only like $\Sigma_p$. Below
the smaller root the modes look less spread than the prior claims, the rule narrows the
prior, and the narrower prior pulls the modes in further. The rule feeds back on
itself.

::: derivation The posterior-averaged rule in the linear case
The posterior variance is $\Sigma_{\text{post}} = \Sigma_p\Sigma_u/(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$, the
reciprocal of the summed precisions, as derived in [lesson:2.4]. The posterior average
of $(d - d_p)^2$ is $(\hat d - d_p)^2 + \Sigma_{\text{post}}$. The drift vanishes where

$$
\frac{\Sigma_p^2(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u)}{(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2} + \frac{\Sigma_p\Sigma_u}{\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u} \;=\; \Sigma_p .
$$

Multiply by $(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2/\Sigma_p$:
$\Sigma_p(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u) + \Sigma_u(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u) = (\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2$.
Expanding both sides and cancelling $\Sigma_u^2$ and $2\Sigma_p\Sigma_u$ leaves
$\Sigma_p\Sigma_p^{*} = \Sigma_p^2$, whose positive solution is $\Sigma_p = \Sigma_p^{*}$.
:::

With $\Sigma_p^{*} = {{lin_s:.0f}}$ and $\Sigma_u = {{lin_vu}}$, the roots of
[eq:lin-roots] are ${{lin_root_lo:.6f}}$ and ${{lin_root_hi:.6f}}$; the larger differs
from the truth by ${{lin_root_hi_pct:.1f}}$ per cent. A simulation in `values.py` with
the log-variance step of [lesson:3.2], started at $\Sigma_p = {{var_prior:.0f}}$,
averages ${{lin_sim_point:.3f}}$ under the point rule and ${{lin_sim_post:.3f}}$ under
the posterior-averaged rule. With $\Sigma_u = {{lin_vu_bad}}$, so that
$3\Sigma_u = {{lin_bad_threshold}}$ exceeds $\Sigma_p^{*}$, the point rule takes the
variance from ${{var_prior:.0f}}$ to ${{lin_bad_after:sci1}}$ in ${{lin_bad_n}}$
trials.

## The missing term

The point rule prefers a smaller variance because of what $\hat\ell$ is.

::: derivation The settled log joint against the evidence
The posterior is Gaussian, so the joint at any $d$ is the evidence times the posterior
density there: $p(d, u) = p(u)\,\mathcal N(d; \hat d, \Sigma_{\text{post}})$. At the
mode the density is $1/\sqrt{2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}}}$, so

$$
\ln p(u; \Sigma_p) \;=\; \hat\ell(u; \Sigma_p) \;+\; \tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}}) .
$$ {#laplace-exact}

Average over the world. Under the model, $u \sim \mathcal N(d_p, \Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$,
and under the world $\mathbb E_{P^*}[(u - d_p)^2] = \Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u$, so

$$
\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\ln p(u; \Sigma_p)\big] \;=\; -\frac{\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u}{2(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)} - \tfrac12\ln\big(2\pi(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)\big) .
$$

This is largest where its derivative,
$(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u)/\big(2(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2\big) - 1/\big(2(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)\big)$,
vanishes, which is at $\Sigma_p = \Sigma_p^{*}$. Subtracting
$\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}})$, with
$\ln\Sigma_{\text{post}} = \ln\Sigma_p + \ln\Sigma_u - \ln(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$, gives

$$
\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\hat\ell(u; \Sigma_p)\big] \;=\; -\frac{\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u}{2(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)} - \tfrac12\ln\Sigma_p - \ln(2\pi) - \tfrac12\ln\Sigma_u .
$$

Its derivative in $\Sigma_p$ is the point rule's drift computed above, as the
envelope theorem of [lesson:3.1] says it must be.
:::

The two differ by $\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}})$. As $\Sigma_p \to 0$ the
evidence falls, because a prior with no spread cannot account for the spread in $u$.
The settled log joint rises without bound, because the prior's density at its own peak
grows like $1/\sqrt{\Sigma_p}$ and the mode moves to that peak. The term
$\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}})$ is the log of the posterior's width, and adding
it cancels the growth.

## The source of the bias

Both rules use the same per-trial derivative. Write it as a function of the state,
$\psi(d) = \partial\ell(d, u; \omega)/\partial\omega$, for one trial and the current
parameters. The point rule uses $\psi(\hat d)$; the posterior-averaged rule uses
$\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[\psi(d)]$. Write $m = \mathbb E_{d\mid u}[d]$ for the posterior mean, $m$ being Week&nbsp;2's
placeholder for a Gaussian's mean put to the same use, and $V$ for the posterior
variance.

::: derivation The gap between the two rules on one trial
Expand $\psi$ about the mode to second order,

$$
\psi(d) \;\approx\; \psi(\hat d) + \psi'(\hat d)\,(d - \hat d) + \tfrac12 \psi''(\hat d)\,(d - \hat d)^2 ,
$$

and average over the posterior. The first moment is
$\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[d - \hat d] = m - \hat d$. The second is
$\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[(d - \hat d)^2] = \mathbb E_{d\mid u}[(d - m + m - \hat d)^2] = V + (m - \hat d)^2$,
the cross term vanishing because $d - m$ averages to zero. So

$$
\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[\psi(d)] - \psi(\hat d) \;\approx\; \psi'(\hat d)\,(m - \hat d) \;+\; \tfrac12 \psi''(\hat d)\,\big[V + (m - \hat d)^2\big] ,
$$ {#gap}

with equality when $\psi$ is a polynomial of degree at most two in $d$.
:::

The posterior-averaged rule has zero drift at the truth, so the point rule's drift
there is minus the process average of [eq:gap]. Two features of the posterior drive it:
the offset of the mode from the mean, which a skewed posterior has, and the posterior
variance, which every posterior has. The derivatives of [lesson:3.2] and [lesson:3.3]
say how each parameter responds.

- **Prior location.** $\psi = (d - d_p)/\Sigma_p - \zeta_p$ is linear in $d$, so
  [eq:gap] is exact and equals $(m - \hat d)/\Sigma_p$. Only the offset matters. In the
  linear model of [lesson:3.1] the posterior is Gaussian, $m = \hat d$, and the location
  is learned without bias. In Week&nbsp;2's model the link flattens with distance, the
  posterior has a long tail towards far sources, the mode lies below the mean, and the
  learned location is too small.
- **Prior variance.** $\psi$ is quadratic in $d$ with $\psi'' = 1/\Sigma_p^2$, so [eq:gap] is
  exact. Since $\psi$ is $(d - d_p)^2/(2\Sigma_p^2)$ plus terms that do not depend on $d$,
  the same gap can also be written directly as $\big[\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[(d - d_p)^2] - (\hat d - d_p)^2\big]/(2\Sigma_p^2)$,
  and $\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[(d - d_p)^2] = V + (m - d_p)^2$ by the same cross-term
  argument, so the bracket is
  $V + (m - d_p)^2 - (\hat d - d_p)^2$. The first term is positive on every trial: the
  modes leave out each posterior's own variance. The other two compare the mean's
  squared deviation with the mode's, and either can be the larger. At $u = {{sqdev_u:.0f}}$
  under Week&nbsp;2's model the mode's squared deviation from $d_p$ is
  ${{sqdev_mode:.4f}}$ and the posterior average of $(d - d_p)^2$ is only
  ${{sqdev_post:.4f}}$, so on that trial the point rule's squared deviation is the
  larger. Averaged over the process, the law of total variance splits the
  posterior-averaged term into $\mathbb E_{P^*}[V]$ and the spread of the posterior
  means $m$ about $d_p$; the point rule has neither, only the spread of the modes. The
  quadrature over the world gives the net effect: the learned variance comes out too
  small.
- **Sensory variance.** $\psi$ depends on $d$ through $\delta_u^2/(2\Sigma_u^2)$, and
  $\mathrm d^2\delta_u^2/\mathrm dd^2 = 2g'(d)^2 - 2\delta_u g''(d)$. This $\psi$ is not a
  polynomial in $d$, so [eq:gap] is an approximation. Where the residual at the mode is
  small, $\psi'' > 0$ and the variance term has the same sign as for the prior. The
  quadrature in the table confirms the sign: the learned sensory variance is too
  small.
- **Source power.** $\psi = \varepsilon_u h(d)$, and at a small residual $\psi' > 0$ while
  $\psi'' < 0$ (an exercise asks for both). This $\psi$ is not a polynomial either, and
  the two terms of [eq:gap] pull in opposite directions. The full calculation puts the
  learned power ${{stat_th_pct_w2:.1f}}$ per cent from the truth in the world equal to
  Week&nbsp;2's model, an overestimate, and ${{stat_th_pct:.1f}}$ per cent from it in the
  louder world of [lesson:3.3], an underestimate.

The second-order formula does not settle the power's sign. Averaged over the louder
world, at the true power, the offset term of [eq:gap] is ${{gap_off_loud:.4f}}$ and the
variance term ${{gap_var_loud:.4f}}$. Their sum, ${{gap_two_loud:.4f}}$, is negative,
while the exact average of the gap, computed from the whole posterior, is
${{gap_exact_loud:.4f}}$, positive; the point rule's drift at the truth is minus that,
and the power is underestimated. The terms the expansion drops reverse the sign. In
the world equal to Week&nbsp;2's model the sum is ${{gap_two_w2:.4f}}$ against an exact
${{gap_exact_w2:.4f}}$: the signs agree and the sizes do not. Where $\psi$ is a
polynomial of degree at most two, as for the prior's location and variance, [eq:gap]
is exact and names the two contributions. Elsewhere it is a local approximation that
names the same two contributions without fixing their net effect, and the signs and
sizes in the table come from the full calculation over the world.

::: derivation What the settled log joint adds to the evidence
For any model and any $u$, factor the joint at its maximum:
$p(u) = \int_0^\infty p(d, u)\,\mathrm dd = e^{\hat\ell}\int_0^\infty e^{\ell(d) - \hat\ell}\,\mathrm dd$.
Since $p(\hat d \mid u) = e^{\hat\ell}/p(u)$,

$$
\hat\ell(u; \omega) \;=\; \ln p(u; \omega) \;+\; \ln p(\hat d \mid u; \omega) .
$$ {#hat-ev}
:::

The settled log joint is the log evidence plus the log of the posterior's height at
its peak. In the linear model that height is $1/\sqrt{2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}}}$, which
is [eq:laplace-exact] again. Climbing $\hat\ell$ climbs the evidence and, at the same
time, rewards any parameter change that makes the posterior taller and narrower,
whether or not it explains the sounds better. That is the general reason the point
rule shrinks variances, and the reason it can run away to zero when nothing else
holds them up.

::: warning The bias comes from the states
The rules are biased because the state is replaced by its mode before the parameter
update is computed. The same rules, applied to the posterior average of the state's
contribution, have zero drift at the truth. A good parameter learner on top of a point
estimate of the state is not enough. The mode leaves out the posterior's spread, and
where the posterior is asymmetric it is also not the posterior mean; how much each
omission costs depends on how the parameter's derivative depends on the state. Of the
four parameters computed here, the sensory variance is off by the most.
:::

::: warning Point estimates of parameters
The animal also holds each parameter as a single number. That is a separate
simplification, and it costs something different: the animal has no measure of how
well it knows a parameter, so a value learned from ten sounds and one learned from
ten thousand are used with the same confidence. With many trials the uncertainty about
a parameter shrinks, and holding only its best value loses little. Early in learning,
or after the world changes, it loses a great deal. Week&nbsp;11 keeps counts for the
parameters of discrete models, which is the simplest way to carry that uncertainty.
:::

## Expectation maximisation

The posterior-averaged rule is a stochastic, online form of an algorithm statisticians
use to fit models with unobserved variables, given its general form by
[Dempster, Laird and Rubin (1977)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1977.tb01600.x).

::: definition Expectation maximisation
**Expectation maximisation**, or EM, alternates two steps from the current parameter
$\omega_{\text{old}}$:

- the E step computes the posterior $p(d \mid u; \omega_{\text{old}})$;
- the M step chooses the new parameter to maximise
  $\mathbb E_{p(d \mid u;\, \omega_{\text{old}})}\big[\ell(d, u; \omega)\big]$, the log joint
  averaged over that fixed posterior.
:::

At $\omega = \omega_{\text{old}}$ the gradient of the M step's objective is the
posterior average of $\partial\ell/\partial\omega$, which by [eq:fisher] is the slope of
the log evidence. EM is therefore an ascent method on the evidence; Week&nbsp;4 proves
that each full EM iteration never lowers it.

The animal's scheme departs from EM in three ways:

1. it replaces the posterior by its mode in the E step;
2. it takes one small gradient step in the M step instead of maximising;
3. it does both one trial at a time.

The second and third change the speed and the noise of learning but not where the
drift vanishes: by [lesson:3.1], a run with a small fixed rate fluctuates about that
zero by order $\sqrt\beta$, with a stationary mean that the drift's curvature can
displace by order $\beta$. The first changes where the drift vanishes, and that is the
bias computed above.
[Buckley et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmp.2017.09.004), in their
introduction, trace the free energy back to work by Hinton and colleagues in which a
function like it implements a variant of EM: the Helmholtz machine of
[Dayan, Hinton, Neal and Zemel (1995)](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1995.7.5.889), which
fits its parameters by maximising a lower bound on the probability of the
observations, and [Neal and Hinton (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-011-5014-9_12),
who justify incremental variants of EM that update the parameters after each
observation, as the posterior-averaged rule does.

## Hyperpriors

[lesson:3.2] noted that the original model forbade small variances outright. A smooth
alternative is to give the variance a prior of its own. A prior over a parameter of a
prior or likelihood is a **hyperprior**.

::: derivation A hyperprior as imaginary trials
Take the density on $\Sigma > 0$

$$
p(\Sigma) \;\propto\; \Sigma^{-(n_0/2 + 1)}\exp\!\left(-\frac{n_0\Sigma_0}{2\Sigma}\right),
$$

an inverse-gamma density with parameters written so that $n_0 > 0$ and
$\Sigma_0 > 0$ can be read in a moment. Suppose $N$ trials give residuals $\delta_n$ whose
log likelihood in $\Sigma$ is $\sum_n\big[-\delta_n^2/(2\Sigma) - \tfrac12\ln\Sigma\big]$,
up to a constant. Adding the log hyperprior,

$$
-\frac{\sum_n \delta_n^2 + n_0\Sigma_0}{2\Sigma} \;-\; \frac{N + n_0 + 2}{2}\,\ln\Sigma .
$$

The hyperprior has the form of $n_0$ extra trials, each with squared residual
$\Sigma_0$, plus a constant $2$. Setting the derivative in $\Sigma$ to zero,

$$
\frac{\sum_n \delta_n^2 + n_0\Sigma_0}{2\Sigma^2} \;=\; \frac{N + n_0 + 2}{2\Sigma}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Sigma \;=\; \frac{\sum_n \delta_n^2 + n_0\Sigma_0}{N + n_0 + 2} .
$$ {#hyper-opt}
:::

So $n_0$ says how many trials' worth of confidence the hyperprior carries, and
$\Sigma_0$ is its guess at the variance. Without data the optimum is
$n_0\Sigma_0/(n_0 + 2)$; with many trials the data dominate. Even if every residual
is zero, [eq:hyper-opt] gives a positive variance, and the objective is bounded above,
since as $\Sigma \to 0$ the term $-n_0\Sigma_0/(2\Sigma)$ falls to $-\infty$ faster than
$-\ln\Sigma$ rises. This removes the unbounded direction found in [lesson:3.1]'s
warning and in the figure above.

An online learner has no fixed $N$: it goes on learning. To use the hyperprior online,
one has to choose how many trials it is shared across, and that choice is part of the
model, not something the derivation supplies. Shared evenly over a window of $N$
trials, the hyperprior adds
$\tfrac1N\big[n_0\Sigma_0/(2\Sigma) - (n_0/2 + 1)\big]$ to each trial's log-variance
step, the derivative of $\tfrac1N\ln p(\Sigma)$ with respect to $\ln\Sigma$. As $\Sigma \to 0$ that term grows without bound and pushes the variance back up,
which is the smooth version of a floor. A hard floor is the limiting case of a
hyperprior with zero density below the floor and no preference above it.

## Learning the precision of the ear

A precision decides how far the estimate follows each channel.

::: derivation The ear's share
Replace the link near the prior location by its tangent,
$g(d) \approx g(d_p) + g'(d_p)(d - d_p)$. The log joint is then quadratic in $d$, and
setting its derivative to zero,

$$
-\Pi_p(d - d_p) + \Pi_u g'(d_p)\big[u - g(d_p) - g'(d_p)(d - d_p)\big] \;=\; 0 ,
$$

gives

$$
\hat d - d_p \;=\; \frac{\Pi_u g'(d_p)}{\Pi_p + \Pi_u g'(d_p)^2}\,\big(u - g(d_p)\big) .
$$

Multiply by $g'(d_p)$. The left side becomes the change in the predicted intensity,
to the same order, and

$$
g'(d_p)\,(\hat d - d_p) \;=\; \frac{\Pi_u g'(d_p)^2}{\Pi_p + \Pi_u g'(d_p)^2}\,\big(u - g(d_p)\big) .
$$ {#share}
:::

The prediction moves the fraction in [eq:share] of the way from what the prior
predicts to what was heard. Its denominator is the Gauss-Newton curvature of
[lesson:2.4] at the prior location, so the fraction is also the ear's part of the
posterior precision. Call it the **ear's share**. With Week&nbsp;2's values it is ${{share_w2:.3f}}$. Learning $\Sigma_u$ is learning that
number, which is how much to trust the ear. [week:8] develops this into attention, as
the optimisation of precisions.

In the widget the world's sensory variance is $\Sigma_u^{*} = {{w3_vu_world}}$, three
times what the animal believes. The animal learns $\Sigma_u$ alone with
$\beta = {{w3_beta}}$, under either rule, on the same sequence of trials.

::: widget precision-learning | Learning the precision of a noisier ear. The learned sensory variance across trials under the point rule and the posterior-averaged rule, against the world's value. The readout gives the ear's share of the curvature at the start, at the truth, and under each learned value.
:::

The posterior-averaged rule reaches ${{w3_post_final:.4f}}$ by the last trial and
averages ${{w3_post_mean_late:.4f}}$ over the second half. The ear's share should fall
from ${{share_w2:.3f}}$ to ${{share_world3:.3f}}$, and at that run's average it is
${{share_post3:.3f}}$.

The point rule's drift, computed by quadrature over the world, vanishes at
$\Sigma_u = {{w3_stat_point:.4f}}$. That is just above the ${{var_obs}}$ the animal
started with: the drift there is ${{w3_grad_start:.5f}}$ in $\ln\Sigma_u$, positive and
small. At the zero the ear's share is ${{w3_share_stat:.3f}}$, against
${{share_w2:.3f}}$ at the start. A channel three times noisier than the animal believes
leaves its trust in the ear almost where it was. The run in the widget averages
${{w3_point_mean_late:.4f}}$ over its second half, slightly below the start, with a
share of ${{share_point3:.3f}}$; one run with a fixed rate fluctuates about the zero of
the drift, as [lesson:3.1] showed, and this one happens to sit below it.

The reason is the one the linear case showed. The point rule measures residuals at
the mode, and the mode is the state that makes the residual small, subject to the
prior. Part of each trial's noise is absorbed by moving the estimate, so the
residuals the rule sees are smaller than the noise, and the variance learned from them
is too small. The point rule does learn a larger variance in the noisier world,
${{w3_stat_point:.4f}}$ against ${{stat_vu_point:.4f}}$ when the world is
Week&nbsp;2's model, but both are far below the truth, and in the noisier world the
learned value barely moves from where the animal started.
