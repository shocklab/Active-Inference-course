The books on active inference have a gap in them. Not a gap in the ideas: a gap
in the working. Steps get skipped, indices go unexplained, and a derivation that
would take a page is compressed into a line beginning "it can be shown that".

This course does the working. Every result is derived in full, in the order a
person actually needs it, with the algebra on the page rather than in an
appendix. Where the standard texts assert that two expressions are equal, we
prove it. Where an equation is written in vectorised form, we say what every
index runs over and where it went.

The second thing this course does is let you turn the knobs. Nearly every idea
here is a statement about how a quantity responds when something else changes,
and that is a miserable thing to learn from static type. So the figures are
live: move a prior and watch a posterior swing, flatten a likelihood until an
observation stops telling you anything, raise a precision until a filter starts
chasing its own noise. Each week also ships a Python notebook, so you can take
the same models away and break them yourself.

It assumes you are comfortable with multivariable calculus, linear algebra and
probability at roughly the level of a good undergraduate degree, and it assumes
nothing at all about neuroscience or machine learning. It runs for twelve weeks,
it is free, and the source is on GitHub.
