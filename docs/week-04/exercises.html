Problems

Exercise 4.6.1Equality in Gibbs' inequality †

Let $Q$ and $P$ be probability distributions on a finite set $\mathcal{S}$ with $P(s) > 0$ for every $s \in \mathcal{S}$. Use the tangent-line bound $\ln y \le y - 1$ for $y > 0$, with equality only at $y = 1$.

(a) Prove that $D_{\mathrm{KL}}[Q \,\|\, P] \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $Q(s) = P(s)$ for every $s \in \mathcal{S}$.

(b) Deduce that for a fixed generative model $P(o, s)$ with $P(o) > 0$, the free energy $\mathcal{F}[Q, o]$ has its minimum over all distributions on $\mathcal{S}$ at $Q^{*}(s) = P(s \mid o)$ and nowhere else, with minimum value $-\ln P(o)$.

(c) For the two-point variable $Y \in \{1, 9\}$ with weights $Q = (0.50, 0.50)$ from Lesson 1, write the Jensen gap $\ln \mathbb{E}_Q[Y] - \mathbb{E}_Q[\ln Y]$ as a divergence of $Q$ from the distribution proportional to $Q(y)\,y$, and evaluate it.

Solution

(a) Split $\mathcal{S}$ into $\mathcal{S}_{+} = \{s : Q(s) > 0\}$ and $\mathcal{S}_{0} = \{s : Q(s) = 0\}$. With the convention $0 \ln 0 = 0$, the states in $\mathcal{S}_{0}$ contribute nothing to the divergence, and $\sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_{+}} Q(s) = 1$. Apply $\ln y \le y - 1$ to $y_s = P(s)/Q(s) > 0$ for $s \in \mathcal{S}_{+}$:

$$\begin{aligned} -D_{\mathrm{KL}}[Q \,\|\, P] &\;=\; \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_{+}} Q(s)\ln\frac{P(s)}{Q(s)} \\ &\;\le\; \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_{+}} Q(s)\!\left(\frac{P(s)}{Q(s)} - 1\right) \\ &\;=\; \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_{+}} P(s) - 1 \;=\; -\sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_{0}} P(s) \;\le\; 0 . \end{aligned}$$

So $D_{\mathrm{KL}}[Q \,\|\, P] \ge \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_{0}} P(s) \ge 0$. For the divergence to be zero both inequalities must be equalities. The second needs $\sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_{0}} P(s) = 0$, and since every $P(s) > 0$, $\mathcal{S}_{0}$ is empty. The first needs $Q(s)\big[\ln y_s - (y_s - 1)\big] = 0$ for every $s$; each bracket is non-positive and $Q(s) > 0$, so each bracket is zero, and by the equality condition $y_s = 1$, that is $Q(s) = P(s)$. Conversely $Q = P$ makes every term $\ln 1 = 0$.

(b) By the divergence-plus-surprise decomposition of Lesson 1, $\mathcal{F}[Q, o] = -\ln P(o) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[Q(s) \,\|\, P(s \mid o)\big]$. The first term does not depend on $Q$. If $Q$ puts mass on a state with $P(o, s) = 0$, then $\mathcal{F}[Q, o] = +\infty$, so only distributions supported where $P(s \mid o) > 0$ can be minimisers. On that set part (a) applies to the second term, which is zero only at $Q(s) = P(s \mid o)$. So $Q^{*}(s) = P(s \mid o)$ is the unique minimiser and $\mathcal{F}[Q^{*}, o] = -\ln P(o)$.

(c) Let $\tilde P(y) = Q(y)\,y / \mathbb{E}_Q[Y]$. With $\mathbb{E}_Q[Y] = 5$, $\tilde P(1) = 1/10$ and $\tilde P(9) = 9/10$. Then

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}[Q \,\|\, \tilde P] \;=\; \sum_{y} Q(y)\ln\frac{Q(y)}{Q(y)\,y / \mathbb{E}_Q[Y]} \;=\; \sum_{y} Q(y)\big(\ln \mathbb{E}_Q[Y] - \ln y\big) \;=\; \ln \mathbb{E}_Q[Y] - \mathbb{E}_Q[\ln Y] .$$

Numerically it is $1.6094 - 1.0986 = 0.5108$ nats, positive because $Q \ne \tilde P$.

Exercise 4.6.2One step of expectation maximisation by hand †

A binary hidden cause $s \in \{0, 1\}$ has unknown prior probability $\omega = P(s = 1 \mid \omega) \in (0, 1)$, the parameter to be learned. A binary sensor $o$ has $P(o = 1 \mid s = 1) = p_{\mathrm{hit}} = 0.80$ and $P(o = 1 \mid s = 0) = p_{\mathrm{fa}} = 0.20$. Over $N = 10$ independent trials the sensor reports seven ones and three zeros. Write $p_1(\omega) = P(o = 1 \mid \omega) = p_{\mathrm{fa}} + (p_{\mathrm{hit}} - p_{\mathrm{fa}})\,\omega$ and $\ln P(o_{1:10} \mid \omega) = 7\ln p_1(\omega) + 3\ln\big(1 - p_1(\omega)\big)$ for the log evidence of the ten trials. Start from $\omega^{(0)} = 0.30$.

(a) Carry out the exact E-step: compute $q_{\mathrm{on}} = P(s = 1 \mid o = 1, \omega^{(0)})$ and $q_{\mathrm{off}} = P(s = 1 \mid o = 0, \omega^{(0)})$, and check that $-\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}$ with these recognition distributions equals $\ln P(o_{1:10} \mid \omega^{(0)})$.

(b) Minimise $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}$ over $\omega$ with the recognition distributions held fixed to obtain $\omega^{(1)}$, and verify the inequalities of

(c) A fixed point of EM satisfies $\omega = \frac{1}{10}\big(7\,P(s = 1 \mid o = 1, \omega) + 3\,P(s = 1 \mid o = 0, \omega)\big)$. Find every fixed point in $[0, 1]$ from this equation, and check the interior one against the maximum of $\ln P(o_{1:10} \mid \omega)$.

Solution

(a) At $\omega^{(0)} = 0.30$, $p_1(\omega^{(0)}) = p_{\mathrm{fa}} + (p_{\mathrm{hit}} - p_{\mathrm{fa}})\,\omega^{(0)} = 0.38$ and $1 - p_1 = 0.62$, so $\ln P(o_{1:10} \mid \omega^{(0)}) = 7\ln 0.38 + 3\ln 0.62 = -8.2072$ nats. Bayes' rule gives

$$q_{\mathrm{on}} \;=\; \frac{p_{\mathrm{hit}}\,\omega^{(0)}}{p_1(\omega^{(0)})} \;=\; \frac{12}{19} \;=\; 0.6316, \qquad q_{\mathrm{off}} \;=\; \frac{(1 - p_{\mathrm{hit}})\,\omega^{(0)}}{1 - p_1(\omega^{(0)})} \;=\; \frac{3}{31} \;=\; 0.0968 .$$

These are the exact posteriors at $\omega^{(0)}$, so each trial's divergence is zero and $-\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}(\omega^{(0)}) = \ln P(o_{1:10} \mid \omega^{(0)}) = -8.2072$ nats.

(b) With the recognition distributions fixed, the part of $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}$ that depends on $\omega$ is $-\sum_n \mathbb{E}_{Q_n}[\ln P(s_n \mid \omega)]$:

$$-\big(7q_{\mathrm{on}} + 3q_{\mathrm{off}}\big)\ln\omega \;-\; \big(7(1 - q_{\mathrm{on}}) + 3(1 - q_{\mathrm{off}})\big)\ln(1 - \omega).$$

Setting the derivative to zero gives the expected fraction of trials with $s = 1$:

$$\omega^{(1)} \;=\; \frac{7q_{\mathrm{on}} + 3q_{\mathrm{off}}}{10} \;=\; \frac{7 \times 12/19 + 3 \times 3/31}{10} \;=\; 0.4711 .$$

With the recognition distributions still fixed, $-\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}(\omega^{(1)}) = -7.5633$ nats, and the log evidence at the new value is $-7.0761$ nats, so $-8.2072 < -7.5633 < -7.0761$.

(c) With $p_1 = p_1(\omega)$, the two posteriors are $p_{\mathrm{hit}}\omega/p_1$ and $(1 - p_{\mathrm{hit}})\omega/(1 - p_1)$, so the fixed-point equation is

$$\omega \;=\; \frac{7}{10}\,\frac{p_{\mathrm{hit}}\,\omega}{p_1} + \frac{3}{10}\,\frac{(1 - p_{\mathrm{hit}})\,\omega}{1 - p_1}.$$

$\omega = 0$ is a solution. For $\omega > 0$, divide by $\omega$ and write $\bar o = 7/10$ for the observed fraction of ones:

$$1 \;=\; \frac{\bar o\, p_{\mathrm{hit}}}{p_1} + \frac{(1 - \bar o)(1 - p_{\mathrm{hit}})}{1 - p_1}.$$

Multiply by $p_1(1 - p_1)$, which is positive for $\omega \in (0, 1]$ since $0 < p_{\mathrm{fa}} < p_{\mathrm{hit}} < 1$:

$$p_1 - p_1^2 \;=\; \bar o\, p_{\mathrm{hit}}\,(1 - p_1) + (1 - \bar o)(1 - p_{\mathrm{hit}})\,p_1 .$$

The coefficient of $p_1$ on the right is $-\bar o\, p_{\mathrm{hit}} + 1 - \bar o - p_{\mathrm{hit}} + \bar o\, p_{\mathrm{hit}} = 1 - \bar o - p_{\mathrm{hit}}$. Moving everything to one side,

$$p_1^2 - (\bar o + p_{\mathrm{hit}})\,p_1 + \bar o\, p_{\mathrm{hit}} \;=\; 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad (p_1 - \bar o)(p_1 - p_{\mathrm{hit}}) \;=\; 0 ,$$

with $\bar o + p_{\mathrm{hit}} = 1.50$ and $\bar o\, p_{\mathrm{hit}} = 0.56$. The root $p_1 = p_{\mathrm{hit}}$ means $p_{\mathrm{fa}} + (p_{\mathrm{hit}} - p_{\mathrm{fa}})\omega = p_{\mathrm{hit}}$, that is $\omega = 1$. The root $p_1 = \bar o$ gives $\omega = (\bar o - p_{\mathrm{fa}})/(p_{\mathrm{hit}} - p_{\mathrm{fa}}) = 5/6 = 0.8333$. So EM has three fixed points in $[0, 1]$: $0$, $5/6$ and $1$, and one in the interior.

The log evidence has derivative $(p_{\mathrm{hit}} - p_{\mathrm{fa}})\big[7/p_1 - 3/(1 - p_1)\big]$, zero only at $p_1 = 7/10$, and it is concave in $p_1$, so its maximum over $(0, 1)$ is at $\omega = 5/6$, the interior fixed point. The endpoints are fixed points because an E-step at $\omega = 0$ or $1$ returns posteriors that put all mass on one value of $s$, and the M-step returns the same endpoint.

Exercise 4.6.3Mean-field approximation of a correlated Gaussian in both directions †

Lesson 5 proved that the product minimising the reverse divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}[Q \,\|\, P]$ from a Gaussian $P$ has factors with variance $1/\Pi_{ii}$, the conditional variance. Take $P = \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with

$$\Sigma \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 & c \\ c & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad c \in (-1, 1).$$

(a) Show that over products $Q = q_1 q_2$ the forward divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}[P \,\|\, Q]$ is minimised by the product of the marginals of $P$, for any $P$ with finite entropies, and that its minimum is the mutual information $I(s_1; s_2)$ under $P$.

(b) For the Gaussian above, compute the minimum forward divergence and compare it with the minimum reverse divergence of Lesson 5. Evaluate the standard deviations of both optimal products and both minima at $c = 0.80$.

(c) Explain in terms of where each divergence weights the logarithm of the ratio why the reverse optimum is narrower than $P$'s marginals and the forward optimum is not.

Solution

(a) Write $P_1, P_2$ for the marginals of $P$. Since $\ln Q(s) = \ln q_1(s_1) + \ln q_2(s_2)$,

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}[P \,\|\, Q] \;=\; -\mathrm{H}[P] - \mathbb{E}_{P}[\ln q_1(s_1)] - \mathbb{E}_{P}[\ln q_2(s_2)] \;=\; -\mathrm{H}[P] + \sum_{i=1}^{2}\Big(\mathrm{H}[P_i] + D_{\mathrm{KL}}[P_i \,\|\, q_i]\Big),$$

using $-\mathbb{E}_{P_i}[\ln q_i] = \mathrm{H}[P_i] + D_{\mathrm{KL}}[P_i \,\|\, q_i]$. Only the two divergences depend on $Q$, and by Gibbs' inequality each is zero exactly at $q_i = P_i$. The minimum is $\mathrm{H}[P_1] + \mathrm{H}[P_2] - \mathrm{H}[P]$, which is $I(s_1; s_2)$: it equals $D_{\mathrm{KL}}[P \,\|\, P_1 P_2]$, the definition of the mutual information as a divergence.

(b) The marginals are $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, with differential entropy $\tfrac12\ln(2\pi e)$ each, and $\mathrm{H}[P] = \tfrac12\ln\big((2\pi e)^2\det\Sigma\big)$ with $\det\Sigma = 1 - c^2$. So

$$\min_{Q} D_{\mathrm{KL}}[P \,\|\, Q] \;=\; \ln(2\pi e) - \tfrac12\ln\big((2\pi e)^2(1 - c^2)\big) \;=\; -\tfrac12\ln(1 - c^2),$$

the same value as the minimum reverse divergence. The optimal products are different: the forward optimum has standard deviation $1$ in each coordinate, the reverse optimum $\sqrt{1 - c^2}$. At $c = 0.80$ these are $1$ and $0.60$, and both minima are $-\tfrac12\ln 0.36 = 0.5108$ nats. The equality of the two minima is a property of the Gaussian, not of the two divergences in general.

(c) The forward divergence averages $\ln(P/Q)$ under $P$, so it is large wherever $P$ has mass and $Q$ has little: an axis-aligned $Q$ must cover the whole tilted ellipse, and the product of the marginals, which spreads over the full width in each coordinate, does so. The reverse divergence averages $\ln(Q/P)$ under $Q$, so it is large wherever $Q$ has mass and $P$ has little: a product as wide as the marginals would put mass in the two corners off the ellipse's long axis, where $P$ is small, so the optimum is narrower and lies inside the ellipse. This is the contrast between the two directions drawn in Week 1, Lesson 4.

Exercise 4.6.4The symmetric noisy-OR fixed point is a saddle

Return to the noisy-OR model of Lesson 2, with both causes having prior probability $p_0 = 0.10$ and posterior entries $P_{ab} = P(s_1 = a, s_2 = b \mid o_1 = 1)$. The coordinate update for either factor is the map $R$ with

$$\ln\frac{R(q)}{1 - R(q)} \;=\; b_0 + b_1\,q ,$$

where $q$ is the probability the other factor gives to its cause being present.

(a) Express $b_0$ and $b_1$ through the likelihood entries $L_{ab} = P(o_1 = 1 \mid s_1 = a, s_2 = b)$ and $p_0$, show that $b_1 < 0$, and prove that $R(q) = q$ has exactly one solution $q_{\mathrm{sad}} \in (0, 1)$.

(b) Show that $\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial q_1 = \ln\frac{q_1}{1 - q_1} - (b_0 + b_1 q_2)$, find the Hessian of $\mathcal{F}(q_1, q_2)$ at $(q_{\mathrm{sad}}, q_{\mathrm{sad}})$, and show that it is indefinite when $R'(q_{\mathrm{sad}}) < -1$, which holds here.

(c) Show that, restricted to the diagonal $q_1 = q_2 = q$, $\mathcal{F}$ has its minimum over $(0, 1)$ at $q_{\mathrm{sad}}$, and conclude that every symmetric product has free energy at least $2.2264$ nats, above the asymmetric minimum $2.2127$.

Solution

(a) From the log-odds form of the update in Lesson 2, the log-odds of $q_1$ given $q_2 = q$ is the prior log-odds plus the expected log-likelihood ratio,

$$\ln\frac{p_0}{1 - p_0} + (1 - q)\ln\frac{L_{10}}{L_{00}} + q\ln\frac{L_{11}}{L_{01}} \;=\; b_0 + b_1 q,$$

so $b_0 = \ln\frac{p_0}{1 - p_0} + \ln\frac{L_{10}}{L_{00}} = 2.3037$ and

$$b_1 \;=\; \ln\frac{L_{11}\,L_{00}}{L_{10}\,L_{01}} \;=\; -4.4066.$$

$b_1$ compares the gain in likelihood from switching on the first cause when the second is already on, $L_{11}/L_{01}$, with the gain when it is off, $L_{10}/L_{00}$. With one cause on, the sensor is already likely to fire, so the second gain is the smaller and $b_1 < 0$: this is explaining away. Then $R(q) = 1/(1 + e^{-(b_0 + b_1 q)})$ is strictly decreasing, so $R(q) - q$ is strictly decreasing and continuous on $[0, 1]$, positive at $q = 0$ (where it equals $R(0) > 0$) and negative at $q = 1$ (where it equals $R(1) - 1 < 0$). It has exactly one zero, $q_{\mathrm{sad}} = 0.5119$.

(b) $\mathcal{F}(q_1, q_2)$ is the sum over the four states of $Q\ln Q - Q\ln P$ with $Q_{ab} = q_1^a(1 - q_1)^{1-a}q_2^b(1 - q_2)^{1-b}$. Differentiating in $q_1$, the entropy part gives $\ln\frac{q_1}{1 - q_1}$ and the energy part gives minus the difference of the expected log joint between $s_1 = 1$ and $s_1 = 0$, which is the log-odds $b_0 + b_1 q_2$ (the posterior and the joint differ by $P(o_1 = 1)$, which cancels in the difference). So $\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial q_1 = \ln\frac{q_1}{1 - q_1} - (b_0 + b_1 q_2)$, and by symmetry the same with the indices exchanged. Differentiating again, at $(q_{\mathrm{sad}}, q_{\mathrm{sad}})$ with $v = q_{\mathrm{sad}}(1 - q_{\mathrm{sad}})$,

$$\nabla^2\mathcal{F} \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 1/v & -b_1 \\ -b_1 & 1/v \end{pmatrix},$$

with eigenvalue $1/v - b_1$ along $(1, 1)$ and $1/v + b_1$ along $(1, -1)$. Since $R'(q) = R(q)(1 - R(q))\,b_1$, at the fixed point $R'(q_{\mathrm{sad}}) = v\,b_1 = -1.1010$, and

$$\frac{1}{v} + b_1 \;=\; \frac{1 + R'(q_{\mathrm{sad}})}{v} \;<\; 0, \qquad \frac{1}{v} - b_1 \;=\; \frac{1 - R'(q_{\mathrm{sad}})}{v} \;>\; 0 .$$

One positive and one negative eigenvalue: the Hessian is indefinite, and the point is a saddle, rising along the diagonal and falling across it.

(c) On the diagonal, by the chain rule and part (b),

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}q}\,\mathcal{F}(q, q) \;=\; 2\Big(\ln\frac{q}{1 - q} - b_0 - b_1 q\Big).$$

Since $b_1 < 0$, the bracket is strictly increasing in $q$; it is zero only where $\ln\frac{q}{1-q} = b_0 + b_1 q$, that is at $q = R(q)$, so at $q_{\mathrm{sad}}$. It is negative below and positive above, so $\mathcal{F}(q, q)$ falls to $q_{\mathrm{sad}}$ and rises after it: its minimum over the diagonal is $\mathcal{F}(q_{\mathrm{sad}}, q_{\mathrm{sad}}) = 2.2264$ nats, above the asymmetric minimum $2.2127$. Week 1, Lesson 4 reached the same conclusion from the second derivative of the divergence along the diagonal; here it follows from the coordinate-ascent map.

Exercise 4.6.5Gaussian variational inference and Laplace on an asymmetric potential †

A hidden state $x \in \mathbb{R}$ has unnormalised joint density $\exp\big(-V(x)\big)$ with

$$V(x) \;=\; \tfrac12 x^2 + e^{x},$$

so $\ell(x) = -V(x)$ is the log joint and the surprise is $-\ln\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-V(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x = 0.0440$ nats, computed by numerical integration. A Gaussian $Q(x) = \mathcal{N}(x; m, \Sigma_q)$ on the whole line has finite free energy here.

(a) Using $\mathbb{E}_Q[e^{x}] = e^{m + \Sigma_q/2}$, write the free energy $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{G}}(m, \Sigma_q)$ of the Gaussian family in closed form.

(b) Show that at its minimum $(\hat m, \hat\Sigma_q)$,

$$\hat\Sigma_q \;=\; \frac{1}{1 - \hat m}, \qquad \hat m + \exp\!\Big(\hat m + \frac{1}{2(1 - \hat m)}\Big) \;=\; 0 .$$

(c) Compute the Laplace free energy at the mode $\hat x$, where $\hat x + e^{\hat x} = 0$, compare both free energies with the surprise, and use the leading correction to Laplace's method from Lesson 3 to predict the sign of the Laplace error.

Solution

(a) Under $Q$, $\mathbb{E}_Q[x^2] = m^2 + \Sigma_q$, $\mathbb{E}_Q[e^{x}] = e^{m + \Sigma_q/2}$, and $\mathrm{H}[Q] = \tfrac12\ln(2\pi e\,\Sigma_q)$. So $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{G}} = \mathbb{E}_Q[V] - \mathrm{H}[Q]$ is

$$\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{G}}(m, \Sigma_q) \;=\; \tfrac12(m^2 + \Sigma_q) + \exp\!\Big(m + \frac{\Sigma_q}{2}\Big) - \tfrac12\ln(2\pi e\,\Sigma_q).$$
(4.6.1)

(b) The two partial derivatives of (4.6.1) are

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{G}}}{\partial m} \;=\; m + e^{m + \Sigma_q/2}, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{G}}}{\partial\Sigma_q} \;=\; \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 e^{m + \Sigma_q/2} - \frac{1}{2\Sigma_q}.$$

Setting the first to zero gives $e^{m + \Sigma_q/2} = -m$, so $\hat m < 0$. Substituting into the second gives $\tfrac12(1 - \hat m) = 1/(2\hat\Sigma_q)$, so $\hat\Sigma_q = 1/(1 - \hat m)$, and substituting that into the first gives the scalar equation stated. Its root is $\hat m = -0.6812$, with $\hat\Sigma_q = 0.5948$ and standard deviation $0.7712$, against the exact posterior mean $-0.6781$ and standard deviation $0.7881$.

(c) At $\hat x = -0.5671$, $V''(\hat x) = 1 + e^{\hat x} = 1 - \hat x$, so the Laplace variance is $1/(1 - \hat x) = 0.6381$ and

$$\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Lap}}(\hat x) \;=\; V(\hat x) - \tfrac12\ln\big(2\pi/(1 - \hat x)\big) \;=\; 0.0337 \text{ nats},$$

which is below the surprise $0.0440$ by $0.0104$ nats. The Gaussian family gives $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{G}}(\hat m, \hat\Sigma_q) = 0.0515$ nats, above the surprise by $0.0075$, as every free energy of a density must be.

Lesson 3 expanded the error of Laplace's method as $\ln P - \ln P_{\mathrm{Lap}} \approx \ell''''/(8\ell''^2) + 5\ell'''^2/(24|\ell''|^3)$ at the mode. Here $\ell'' = -(1 + e^{\hat x})$ and $\ell''' = \ell'''' = -e^{\hat x}$, so the two terms are $-0.0289$ and $0.0174$, summing to $-0.0115$. Since $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Lap}} - (-\ln P) = \ln P - \ln P_{\mathrm{Lap}}$, the prediction is that the Laplace free energy lies below the surprise by about $-0.0115$ in signed terms; the computed difference is $-0.0104$. The quartic term, negative because $\ell'''' < 0$, outweighs the cubic one.

Exercise 4.6.6Changes of coordinate †

Let $\chi : \mathbb{R} \to (0, \infty)$ be a smooth bijection with $\chi'(\rho) > 0$, and write $d = \chi(\rho)$. A density $Q_\rho$ on $\mathbb{R}$ induces the density $Q_d(d) = Q_\rho(\chi^{-1}(d))/\chi'(\chi^{-1}(d))$ on $(0, \infty)$, and the joint density transforms by the same rule, $P_\rho(u, \rho) = P_d(u, \chi(\rho))\,\chi'(\rho)$ (Week 2, Lesson 2).

(a) Prove that $\mathcal{F}[Q_\rho, u] = \mathcal{F}[Q_d, u]$, although the differential entropies differ by $\mathbb{E}_{Q_\rho}[\ln\chi'(\rho)]$.

(b) Let $\ell_d(d) = \ln P_d(u, d)$ have a nondegenerate maximum at $\hat d$, and $\ell_\rho(\rho) = \ln P_\rho(u, \rho)$. Show that $\chi^{-1}(\hat d)$ is a stationary point of $\ell_\rho$ if and only if $\chi''$ vanishes there. Show that for $\chi(\rho) = e^{\rho}(\rho^2 - 2\rho + 3)$ and a model with $\hat d = \chi(-1)$, the two Laplace free energies are equal. Then evaluate both on Week 2's sound model with $\chi(\rho) = e^{\rho}$.

(c) Connect (a) and (b) to the log-normal family of Lesson 3.

Solution

(a) With $d = \chi(\rho)$, $\mathrm{d}d = \chi'(\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\rho$ and $Q_d(\chi(\rho))\,\chi'(\rho) = Q_\rho(\rho)$, so

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}[Q_d, u] &\;=\; \int_0^\infty Q_d(d)\ln\frac{Q_d(d)}{P_d(u, d)}\,\mathrm{d}d \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^\infty Q_d(\chi(\rho))\,\chi'(\rho)\,\ln\frac{Q_d(\chi(\rho))\,\chi'(\rho)}{P_d(u, \chi(\rho))\,\chi'(\rho)}\,\mathrm{d}\rho \\ &\;=\; \int_{-\infty}^\infty Q_\rho(\rho)\ln\frac{Q_\rho(\rho)}{P_\rho(u, \rho)}\,\mathrm{d}\rho \;=\; \mathcal{F}[Q_\rho, u], \end{aligned}$$

where the second step multiplies the numerator and denominator of the ratio by $\chi'(\rho)$. Separately, $\mathrm{H}[Q_d] = -\int Q_\rho\ln\big(Q_\rho/\chi'\big)\,\mathrm{d}\rho = \mathrm{H}[Q_\rho] + \mathbb{E}_{Q_\rho}[\ln\chi'(\rho)]$, and the expected energy changes by the same amount, so the difference is unchanged.

(b) $\ell_\rho(\rho) = \ell_d(\chi(\rho)) + \ln\chi'(\rho)$, so

$$\ell_\rho'(\rho) \;=\; \ell_d'(\chi(\rho))\,\chi'(\rho) + \frac{\chi''(\rho)}{\chi'(\rho)} .$$

At $\rho_0 = \chi^{-1}(\hat d)$ the first term is zero, so $\ell_\rho'(\rho_0) = \chi''(\rho_0)/\chi'(\rho_0)$, which is zero exactly when $\chi''(\rho_0) = 0$. Where $\chi''(\rho_0) \ne 0$ the mode in $\rho$ is at a different physical state.

For $\chi(\rho) = e^{\rho}(\rho^2 - 2\rho + 3)$: $\rho^2 - 2\rho + 3 = (\rho - 1)^2 + 2 > 0$, $\chi \to 0$ as $\rho \to -\infty$ and $\chi \to \infty$ as $\rho \to \infty$, and $\chi'(\rho) = e^{\rho}(\rho^2 + 1) > 0$, so $\chi$ is a bijection onto $(0, \infty)$. Further, $\chi''(\rho) = e^{\rho}(\rho + 1)^2$ and $\chi'''(\rho) = e^{\rho}(\rho + 1)(\rho + 3)$, both zero at $\rho_0 = -1$. Differentiating $\ell_\rho'$ again,

$$\ell_\rho''(\rho) \;=\; \ell_d''(\chi)\,\chi'^2 + \ell_d'(\chi)\,\chi'' + \frac{\chi'''}{\chi'} - \Big(\frac{\chi''}{\chi'}\Big)^2,$$

which at $\rho_0$ is $\ell_d''(\hat d)\,\chi'(\rho_0)^2 < 0$. So $\rho_0$ is a nondegenerate maximum of $\ell_\rho$, and

$$\mathcal{F}^{(\rho)}_{\mathrm{Lap}} \;=\; -\ell_d(\hat d) - \ln\chi'(\rho_0) - \tfrac12\ln\frac{2\pi}{|\ell_d''(\hat d)|\,\chi'(\rho_0)^2} \;=\; -\ell_d(\hat d) - \tfrac12\ln\frac{2\pi}{|\ell_d''(\hat d)|} \;=\; \mathcal{F}^{(d)}_{\mathrm{Lap}} .$$

So a nonlinear change of coordinates need not change the Laplace free energy. In general it does. On Week 2's sound model with $\rho = \ln d$, $\chi'' = e^{\rho} \ne 0$, and the mode of $\ell_\rho$ is at $\hat\rho = 0.6306$, the physical state $e^{\hat\rho} = 1.8787$ against $\hat d = 1.5500$. The Laplace free energy in $\rho$ is $0.0564$ nats, against $0.4325$ in $d$ and the surprise $0.3263$: the first lies below the surprise and the second above it.

(c) For $\chi(\rho) = e^{\rho}$, $\ln\chi'(\rho) = \rho$, so part (a) gives $\mathrm{H}[Q_d] = \tfrac12\ln(2\pi e\,\Sigma_\rho) + m_\rho$ for a Gaussian $Q_\rho$ with mean $m_\rho$ and variance $\Sigma_\rho$, the log-normal entropy of Lesson 3. The log-normal free energy, $0.3588$ nats on the sound model, is the free energy of an actual density, the same whether computed in $\rho$ or in $d$, and lies above the surprise by $0.0324$ nats. The Laplace free energy is computed with a quadratic approximation to the log joint, is generally different from the exact free energy of the Laplace recognition density for the original model, and, as part (b) shows, depends on the coordinate in which the quadratic expansion is made.

Exercise 4.6.7The two-cause model: fixed spread and averaged rates

In the model of Lesson 5, $s_i \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda_i)$, $u_1 = s_2 s_1^2 + z_1$ and $u_2 = s_1 s_2^2 + z_2$ with standard normal noise.

(a) For Gamma factors with means $m_1, m_2$ and a common shape $k$, use $\mathbb{E}_q[s^2] = m^2 c_2(k)$ and $\mathbb{E}_q[s^4] = m^4 c_4(k)$ from Lesson 5 to show that the coefficient of variation of the predicted sensor output $s_2 s_1^2$ satisfies $\mathrm{CV}_Q[s_2 s_1^2]^2 = (5k + 6)/k^2$, whatever the means. Evaluate it at $k = 1$ and as $k \to \infty$.

(b) With Exponential factors, each trial's $q_{n,i}$ has mean $m_{n,i}$ and rate $1/m_{n,i}$. Show that the M-step of Lesson 5 is the harmonic mean of the trial rates and that the averaging rule is their arithmetic mean. For two trials whose rates are $r$ and $r/2$, compute both and their ratio.

(c) Using the table in Lesson 5, state which effect each of these three comparisons measures, and why the third is larger than the second: Exponential with the M-step against Gamma, one step from the true rates; the averaging rule against the M-step, one step from the true rates; the same two rules at their fixed points.

Solution

(a) By independence, $\mathbb{E}_Q[s_2 s_1^2] = m_2\,m_1^2 c_2(k)$ and $\mathbb{E}_Q[(s_2 s_1^2)^2] = m_2^2 c_2(k)\,m_1^4 c_4(k)$. Dividing the variance by the squared mean cancels $m_1$ and $m_2$:

$$\mathrm{CV}_Q[s_2 s_1^2]^2 \;=\; \frac{c_2(k)\,c_4(k)}{c_2(k)^2} - 1 \;=\; \frac{c_4(k)}{c_2(k)} - 1 \;=\; \frac{(k + 2)(k + 3)}{k^2} - 1 \;=\; \frac{5k + 6}{k^2}.$$

At $k = 1$ this is $11$, so the coefficient of variation is $\sqrt{11} = 3.3166$, the value fixed by the Exponential family. As $k \to \infty$ it behaves as $5/k \to 0$.

(b) The M-step is $\lambda_i = \big(\frac1N\sum_n m_{n,i}\big)^{-1}$. Since $m_{n,i} = 1/r_{n,i}$ for the trial rates $r_{n,i}$, this is $\big(\frac1N\sum_n 1/r_{n,i}\big)^{-1}$, the harmonic mean. The averaging rule is $\frac1N\sum_n r_{n,i}$, the arithmetic mean. For rates $r$ and $r/2$: the arithmetic mean is $3r/4$, the harmonic mean is $2/(1/r + 2/r) = 2r/3$, and their ratio is $9/8$.

(c) Exponential against Gamma, one step from $\lambda^{*}$, measures the fixed coefficient of variation (effect 1): $100$ and $114$ per cent on this sample. The averaging rule against the M-step, one step from $\lambda^{*}$, measures effect 2: $13.0$ and $17.0$ per cent. At the fixed points the difference is $20.5$ and $26.2$ per cent. It is larger because the loop multiplies each rule's one-step displacement (effect 3), and the averaging rule starts from a larger one. The comparisons hold on this sample and these true rates, not on every trial or every sample.
