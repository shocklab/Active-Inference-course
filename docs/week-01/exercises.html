Problems

Exercise 1.6.1A single alarm

An internal temperature sensor reports either an alarm or no alarm. The body is in one of two states, overheated or normal. The prior probability of overheating is $0.02$. The sensor raises the alarm with probability $0.90$ when the body is overheated and with probability $0.05$ when it is normal.

(a) Write down the likelihood matrix $\mathbf{A}$, with observations down the side and states across the top as in Lesson 3, and check which of its rows and columns sum to one.

(b) The alarm sounds. Compute the evidence, the posterior probability of overheating, and the surprise.

(c) The odds of an event with probability $p$ are $p/(1-p)$. Show that, for two states $s_1$ and $s_2$ and an observation with $P(o \mid s_2) > 0$, Bayes' theorem can be written as

$$\frac{P(s_1 \mid o)}{P(s_2 \mid o)} = \frac{P(o \mid s_1)}{P(o \mid s_2)} \cdot \frac{P(s_1)}{P(s_2)},$$

posterior odds equal the likelihood ratio times the prior odds, and redo (b) this way.

(d) A second alarm sounds, conditionally independent of the first given the state. Find the posterior probability of overheating using the odds form.

Solution

(a) With rows (alarm, no alarm) and columns (overheated, normal),

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.90 & 0.05 \\ 0.10 & 0.95 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The columns sum to one, since each is the distribution of the report given one state. The rows do not: the alarm row sums to $0.90 + 0.05$ and the no-alarm row to $0.10 + 0.95$, and neither has any reason to sum to one.

(b) The numerators $P(o \mid s)P(s)$ of Bayes' theorem are

$$0.90 \times 0.02 = 0.018, \qquad 0.05 \times (1 - 0.02) = 0.049 .$$

Their sum is the evidence, $P(\text{alarm}) = 0.067$, so the posterior probability of overheating is $0.018/0.067 = 0.2687$, and the surprise is $-\ln 0.067 = 2.703$ nats.

A sensor that catches nine overheatings in ten leaves overheating less likely than not after it fires, at about 27%. The reason is the prior: normal states are so common that their occasional false alarms outnumber the true ones, $0.049$ against $0.018$. In whole numbers: of 10,000 bodies, 200 are overheated and 180 of those raise the alarm; 9,800 are normal and 490 of those raise it too. Of the 670 alarms, 180 come from overheated bodies, and $180/670 = 0.2687$.

(c) Write Bayes' theorem for each state and divide:

$$\frac{P(s_1 \mid o)}{P(s_2 \mid o)} = \frac{P(o \mid s_1)P(s_1)/P(o)}{P(o \mid s_2)P(s_2)/P(o)} = \frac{P(o \mid s_1)}{P(o \mid s_2)} \cdot \frac{P(s_1)}{P(s_2)} .$$

The evidence cancels. With only two states, $P(s_2) = 1 - P(s_1)$ and $P(s_2 \mid o) = 1 - P(s_1 \mid o)$, so the two ratios of state probabilities are odds in the sense just defined. Here the prior odds are $0.02/(1-0.02) = 1/49$ and the likelihood ratio is $0.90/0.05 = 18$, so the posterior odds are $18/49 = 0.3673$. To convert odds back to a probability, solve $\text{odds} = p/(1-p)$ for $p$, which gives $p = \text{odds}/(1+\text{odds})$. Keeping the fraction, $p = (18/49)/(1 + 18/49) = 18/(49 + 18) = 18/67 = 0.2687$, as in (b).

(d) The posterior after the first alarm is the prior for the second, so the odds are multiplied by the same likelihood ratio again:

$$\frac{ 18^2 }{ 49 } = \frac{ 324 }{ 49 } = 6.612, \qquad P(\text{overheated} \mid \text{two alarms}) = \frac{6.612}{1 + 6.612} = 0.869 .$$

Bayes' theorem applied directly to both alarms gives the same answer. The likelihoods of two alarms are $0.90^2$ and $0.05^2$, so the numerators are $0.0162$ and $0.00245$, the evidence is $0.01865$, and the posterior is $0.0162/0.01865 = 0.869$.

The odds form never needs the evidence, and each conditionally independent observation contributes one multiplicative factor. Taking logarithms turns the product into a sum, so in log odds each observation adds its log likelihood ratio. Here the prior log odds are $\ln(1/49) = -3.8918$ and each alarm adds $\ln 18 = 2.8904$, giving $-1.0014$ after one alarm and $1.8889$ after two. The sign changes between them, which is the moment overheating becomes the more probable state. The problem on what the prior decides, below, uses a variant of this form, in which the second hypothesis is "not leopard" and its likelihood is a prior-weighted mixture of the baboon and nothing columns.

Exercise 1.6.2Normalisation, both ways †

Let $\mathbf{A}$ be the likelihood matrix with $A_{ij} = P(o = i \mid s = j)$.

(a) State which of the rows and columns of $\mathbf{A}$ sum to one, and why.

(b) Show that $\sum_i P(o = i) = 1$ follows from that, for any prior $P(s)$.

(c) A colleague builds a model in which the rows of $\mathbf{A}$ have been normalised by mistake. Describe, without computing anything, what goes wrong.

Solution

(a) The columns. Column $j$ is the distribution $P(o \mid s = j)$ over all possible observations given that the state is $j$. It is a probability distribution over $o$ and so must sum to one. A row is $P(o = i \mid s)$ read as a function of $s$: a likelihood, not a distribution, and it has no reason to sum to anything in particular.

(b) Write $P(o = i) = \sum_j A_{ij} P(s = j)$. Then

$$\sum_i P(o = i) = \sum_i \sum_j A_{ij} P(s=j) = \sum_j P(s=j) \sum_i A_{ij} = \sum_j P(s=j) \cdot 1 = 1,$$

where the interchange of the two finite sums is unproblematic and the inner sum is one by (a).

(c) Row-normalising makes $\sum_i A_{ij} \neq 1$ in general, so the columns are no longer distributions over observations. The immediate symptom is that $\sum_i P(o=i) \neq 1$: the evidence no longer defines a probability distribution, so the surprise $-\ln P(o)$ is not comparable across observations and can even be negative. Bayes' theorem still returns a normalised posterior, because the division by $P(o)$ hides the error, which is exactly what makes this mistake hard to find. Check the column sums.

Exercise 1.6.3What the prior decides

Using the leopard, baboon and nothing model from Lesson 3, write $\rho$ for the prior probability that a leopard is present, and find the value of $\rho$ at which observing a tawny flash leaves you exactly undecided between leopard and not-leopard, that is, $P(s = \text{leopard} \mid o) = 1/2$. Keep the baboon and nothing priors in the ratio $22 : 70$ as $\rho$ varies.

Solution

Let the leopard prior be $\rho$. The other two share $1 - \rho$ in the ratio $22:70$, so they are $\tfrac{22}{92}(1-\rho)$ and $\tfrac{70}{92}(1-\rho)$. The tawny-flash row is $(0.70, 0.15, 0.02)$. The posterior odds of leopard against everything else are

$$\frac{0.70\,\rho}{\left(0.15 \cdot \tfrac{22}{92} + 0.02 \cdot \tfrac{70}{92}\right)(1-\rho)} = \frac{0.70\,\rho}{0.051087\,(1-\rho)} .$$

Setting this to 1 gives $0.70\rho = 0.051087(1-\rho)$, so $\rho = 0.051087 / 0.751087 = 0.0680$.

So a leopard prior of about 6.8% is the tipping point: the model in Lesson 3 used $\rho = 0.08$, which is just above it, which is why the posterior came out at $0.544$ rather than exactly a half. The observation is strong, but it is only just strong enough to overturn a prior of that size, and a slightly more sceptical hiker would have remained unconvinced by the same evidence.

Exercise 1.6.4Two observations in a row †

You observe a branch shaking, and then, a moment later, a tawny flash. Assume the two observations are conditionally independent given the state, and that the state has not changed.

(a) Compute the posterior after both observations, starting from the Lesson 3 prior.

(b) Show that you get the same answer whichever order you process them in.

(c) What feature of the model makes (b) true, and give a physical situation in which it would fail.

Solution

(a) After the shaking branch the posterior was $(0.082988, 0.684647, 0.232365)$. Use this as the prior and apply the tawny-flash row $(0.70, 0.15, 0.02)$:

$$(0.70 \times 0.082988,\ 0.15 \times 0.684647,\ 0.02 \times 0.232365) = (0.058091,\ 0.102697,\ 0.004647).$$

The sum is $0.165436$, giving a posterior of $(0.35114,\ 0.62077,\ 0.02809)$.

Baboon is now the leading hypothesis at 62%. The shaking branch was strong evidence for a baboon, and although the tawny flash pulls back towards leopard, it does not pull far enough.

(b) Processing the flash first gives posterior $(0.543689, 0.320388, 0.135922)$, then applying the branch row $(0.25, 0.75, 0.08)$:

$$(0.135922,\ 0.240291,\ 0.010874), \quad \text{sum } 0.387087,$$

which normalises to $(0.35114,\ 0.62077,\ 0.02809)$: identical.

(c) Conditional independence given the state. Under it, the joint likelihood factorises as $P(o_1, o_2 \mid s) = P(o_1 \mid s) P(o_2 \mid s)$, so the posterior is proportional to $P(s) P(o_1\mid s) P(o_2 \mid s)$, and multiplication commutes. It fails whenever one observation influences the next other than through the state. A concrete case: you shout after seeing the branch move. Whatever is in the scrub reacts to the shout, so the second observation depends on the first directly, not only through what the animal is. Sequential active inference agents create exactly this kind of dependence on purpose, which is why Week 9 has to be careful about the difference between the time index on an observation and the time index on a belief.

Exercise 1.6.5When does an observation tell you nothing?

Prove that the posterior equals the prior for every prior if and only if $P(o \mid s)$ does not depend on $s$.

Solution

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $P(o\mid s) = c$ for all $s$, for the observed $o$. Then $P(o) = \sum_s c\,P(s) = c$, and

$$P(s \mid o) = \frac{c\,P(s)}{c} = P(s).$$

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $P(s\mid o) = P(s)$ for every prior. Bayes gives $P(o\mid s)P(s) = P(o)P(s)$ for every $s$, so wherever $P(s) > 0$ we may divide to get $P(o\mid s) = P(o)$, a quantity that does not depend on $s$. Taking a prior with full support forces this at every $s$.

This is the formal content of the ambiguity slider. A sensory channel is useful exactly to the extent that different states disagree about what it will report.

Exercise 1.6.6Posterior entropy

Use the leopard, baboon and nothing model of Lesson 3, with prior $(0.08, 0.22, 0.70)$.

(a) Compute the entropy of the prior, and the entropy of the posterior after each of the three observations. Which observations increase the entropy of the agent's beliefs about the state?

(b) Show that for any finite model

$$\sum_o P(o)\,\mathrm{H}[P(s \mid o)] \;=\; \mathrm{H}[P(s)] \;-\; I(s;o),$$

where $I(s;o)$ is the mutual information defined in Lesson 3.

(c) Check (b) numerically for this model, and explain why your answer to (a) does not contradict it.

Solution

(a) The prior entropy is

$$\mathrm{H}[P(s)] = -(0.08 \ln 0.08 + 0.22 \ln 0.22 + 0.70 \ln 0.70) = 0.78484 \text{ nats}.$$

For comparison, the largest entropy on three states is $\ln 3 = 1.0986$ nats, reached by the uniform distribution.

Each posterior is the numerators of Bayes' theorem divided by the evidence, as in Lesson 3, which gives the tawny-flash case in full and lists the numerators and evidence for the other two:

$$\frac{(0.020,\ 0.165,\ 0.056)}{0.241}, \qquad \frac{(0.004,\ 0.022,\ 0.630)}{0.656}$$

for the shaking branch and for quiet. For the tawny flash the entropy is

$$-(0.5437 \ln 0.5437 + 0.3204 \ln 0.3204 + 0.1359 \ln 0.1359) = 0.96724 \text{ nats},$$

and the same formula gives the other two:

observed $P(o)$ posterior entropy, nats change, nats
tawny flash 0.103 (0.544, 0.320, 0.136) 0.96724 $+0.1824$
branch shakes 0.241 (0.083, 0.685, 0.232) 0.80507 $+0.0202$
quiet 0.656 (0.006, 0.034, 0.960) 0.18380 $-0.6010$

The tawny flash and the shaking branch both increase the entropy. The prior puts most of its mass on "nothing". The tawny flash moves mass from there onto leopard and baboon, and the shaking branch moves it almost entirely onto baboon; either way the belief ends up less concentrated. After a tawny flash the entropy is close to the three-state maximum of $1.0986$. The agent is less certain of the state than before, although the observation moved its beliefs further than any other: its information gain was $0.940$ nats. Entropy measures how spread a belief is, and information gain measures how far it moved, so a large movement away from a concentrated belief can raise both.

(b) Start from the definition of $I(s;o)$ and split the logarithm of the ratio:

$$\begin{aligned} I(s;o) &= \sum_o P(o) \sum_s P(s \mid o) \ln \frac{P(s \mid o)}{P(s)} \\ &= \sum_o P(o) \sum_s P(s \mid o) \ln P(s \mid o) \;-\; \sum_o P(o) \sum_s P(s \mid o) \ln P(s) . \end{aligned}$$

The inner sum of the first term is $-\mathrm{H}[P(s \mid o)]$, so the first term is $-\sum_o P(o)\,\mathrm{H}[P(s \mid o)]$. In the second term, exchange the two finite sums and use the product rule:

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_o P(o) \sum_s P(s \mid o) \ln P(s) &= \sum_s \ln P(s) \sum_o P(o)\,P(s \mid o) \\ &= \sum_s \ln P(s) \sum_o P(s, o) \\ &= \sum_s P(s) \ln P(s) = -\mathrm{H}[P(s)] . \end{aligned}$$

The sums over $o$ run over observations with $P(o) > 0$, for which the posterior is defined. Terms with $P(s \mid o) = 0$ contribute zero under the convention $0 \ln 0 = 0$ of Lesson 1, and $P(s) = 0$ forces $P(s \mid o) = 0$, so no logarithm of zero survives. Together,

$$I(s;o) = \mathrm{H}[P(s)] - \sum_o P(o)\,\mathrm{H}[P(s \mid o)],$$

which rearranges to the identity asked for. In words: the amount by which an observation lowers the entropy of beliefs, averaged over the observations, is the mutual information.

(c) The weighted average of the posterior entropies is

$$\begin{aligned} &0.103 \times 0.9672 + 0.241 \times 0.8051 + 0.656 \times 0.1838 \\ &\quad= 0.0996 + 0.1940 + 0.1206 = 0.4142 \text{ nats}, \end{aligned}$$

and $0.7848 - 0.4142 = 0.3706$. Computing the mutual information directly, as the average of the three information gains of Lesson 3,

$$0.103 \times 0.9396 + 0.241 \times 0.5241 + 0.656 \times 0.2249 = 0.3706 \text{ nats},$$

gives the same value. In the unrounded stored values the two routes differ by less than $10^{-12}$.

Mutual information is an average of KL divergences, each non-negative by the argument in Lesson 2, so $I(s;o) \ge 0$ and the identity gives

$$\sum_o P(o)\,\mathrm{H}[P(s \mid o)] \le \mathrm{H}[P(s)] .$$

On average over the observations the model expects, an observation cannot increase the entropy of beliefs about the state. Nothing constrains a single observation, and (a) has two of three increasing it. They are outweighed by quiet, which the model expects with probability $0.656$ and which lowers the entropy by $0.6010$ nats. The average in this statement is taken under the model's own $P(o)$. If the world produced observations with different frequencies, the average under those frequencies could rise: in a world that produced a tawny flash every time, each update from this prior would raise it.

Exercise 1.6.7The two forms of mutual information †

(a) Show that

$$I(s;o) = \sum_{s,o} P(s,o) \ln \frac{P(s,o)}{P(s)\,P(o)}$$

and

$$I(s;o) = \mathrm{H}[P(o)] - \sum_s P(s)\,\mathrm{H}[P(o \mid s)] .$$

Explain why the first form shows that $I(s;o)$ is symmetric in $s$ and $o$, although each information gain $D_{\mathrm{KL}}[P(s \mid o)\,\|\,P(s)]$ is a statement about states alone.

(b) Evaluate the second form for the leopard model and compare it with the previous problem.

(c) Show that $0 \le I(s;o) \le \min\big(\mathrm{H}[P(s)],\, \mathrm{H}[P(o)]\big)$.

(d) The channel-noise weight $\alpha$ of Lesson 3 replaces $\mathbf{A}$ by $\mathbf{A}(\alpha) = (1-\alpha)\mathbf{A} + \alpha U$, where every entry of $U$ is $1/3$. Compute $I(s;o)$ for the leopard prior at $\alpha = 0.25$, $0.5$ and $0.75$, and compare it with the straight line $(1-\alpha)\,I_0$, where $I_0$ is the value at $\alpha = 0$. Prove that $I(s;o) \le (1-\alpha)\,I_0$ for every $\alpha \in [0,1]$.

Solution

(a) By the product rule, $P(o)\,P(s \mid o) = P(s,o)$ and $P(s \mid o)/P(s) = P(s,o)/[P(s)P(o)]$. Substituting both into the definition,

$$I(s;o) = \sum_o \sum_s P(o)\,P(s \mid o) \ln \frac{P(s \mid o)}{P(s)} = \sum_{s,o} P(s,o) \ln \frac{P(s,o)}{P(s)\,P(o)} .$$

Define $I(o;s)$ by the same recipe with the roles exchanged, $\sum_s P(s)\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}[P(o \mid s)\,\|\,P(o)]$. The same two substitutions turn it into the same double sum, because the joint, the product of marginals and the double sum are all unchanged when the names $s$ and $o$ are swapped. So $I(s;o) = I(o;s)$. The symmetry belongs to the average: a single term $D_{\mathrm{KL}}[P(s \mid o)\,\|\,P(s)]$ has no partner with the roles reversed that it must equal.

For the second form, write the ratio the other way round, $P(s,o)/[P(s)P(o)] = P(o \mid s)/P(o)$, write $P(s,o) = P(s)P(o \mid s)$ by the product rule in the first term, and split the logarithm:

$$\begin{aligned} I(s;o) &= \sum_s P(s) \sum_o P(o \mid s) \ln P(o \mid s) \;-\; \sum_o \ln P(o) \sum_s P(s,o) \\ &= -\sum_s P(s)\,\mathrm{H}[P(o \mid s)] + \mathrm{H}[P(o)] , \end{aligned}$$

using $\sum_s P(s,o) = P(o)$ in the second term. This is the computation of the previous problem with the roles of $s$ and $o$ exchanged.

(b) The evidence vector is $P(o) = (0.103, 0.241, 0.656)$, with entropy $0.8536$ nats. The columns of $\mathbf{A}$ have entropies $0.7460$ (leopard), $0.7306$ (baboon) and $0.3751$ (nothing). Weighting by the prior,

$$0.08 \times 0.7460 + 0.22 \times 0.7306 + 0.70 \times 0.3751 = 0.4830,$$

so $I(s;o) = 0.8536 - 0.4830 = 0.3706$ nats, the value found from the state side in the previous problem. No posterior and no entropy appears in both routes; they share only the joint distribution and the evidence vector computed from it.

(c) The lower bound holds because $I(s;o)$ is a $P(o)$-weighted average of KL divergences, each non-negative by Lesson 2. Every entropy is non-negative, since each term $-p \ln p$ is non-negative for $p \in [0,1]$. The previous problem's identity therefore gives $I(s;o) = \mathrm{H}[P(s)] - \sum_o P(o)\,\mathrm{H}[P(s\mid o)] \le \mathrm{H}[P(s)]$, and the second form in (a) gives $I(s;o) \le \mathrm{H}[P(o)]$ in the same way. For the leopard model, $0.3706$ sits below both $0.7848$ and $0.8536$. An observation cannot tell you more about the state, on average, than you were uncertain about to begin with, nor more than the observation itself varies.

(d) Computed from the definition with $\mathbf{A}(\alpha)$ in place of $\mathbf{A}$:

$\alpha$ $I(s;o)$, nats $(1-\alpha)\,I_0$, nats $I(s;o)/I_0$
0.25 0.1925 0.2780 0.519
0.5 0.0846 0.1853 0.228
0.75 0.0218 0.0927 0.059

with $I_0 = 0.3706$. For this model the information falls faster than the line: mixed halfway towards uniform, the channel keeps 23% of it. The bound proved below guarantees only that it keeps at most half, and other models come closer to that bound.

The idea of the proof is a chord. At $\alpha = 1$ every column of $\mathbf{A}(\alpha)$ is the same, so every observation has the same probability under every state and $I = 0$. If $I$ is a convex function of $\alpha$ along the segment, its graph lies below the straight line joining $(0, I_0)$ to $(1, 0)$, which is $(1-\alpha)I_0$. The log-sum inequality is the tool that supplies the convexity of each KL divergence.

For the proof, use the form of (a) with $P(o \mid s)/P(o)$ in the logarithm and $P(s,o) = P(s)P(o \mid s)$:

$$I(s;o) = \sum_s P(s) \sum_o P(o \mid s) \ln \frac{P(o \mid s)}{P(o)} ,$$

whose inner sum is a KL divergence, so

$$I(s;o) = \sum_s P(s)\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[P(o \mid s) \,\|\, P(o)\big] .$$

Write $p_s$ for column $s$ of $\mathbf{A}$, $q = \sum_s P(s)\,p_s$ for the evidence vector, and $\bar p = (\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$. Under $\mathbf{A}(\alpha)$ the columns are $(1-\alpha)p_s + \alpha \bar p$, and because the prior sums to one the evidence vector is $\sum_s P(s)[(1-\alpha)p_s + \alpha \bar p] = (1-\alpha)q + \alpha \bar p$. So it is enough to show that, for distributions $p$, $q$ and $\bar p$ on the observations,

$$\begin{aligned} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[(1-\alpha)p + \alpha \bar p \,\big\|\, (1-\alpha)q + \alpha \bar p\big] &\le (1-\alpha)\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}[p \,\|\, q] + \alpha\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\bar p \,\|\, \bar p] \\ &= (1-\alpha)\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}[p \,\|\, q] , \end{aligned}$$

and then average over $s$ with weights $P(s)$.

The inequality follows from the log-sum inequality: for non-negative $c_1, c_2$ and positive $\phi_1, \phi_2$,

$$(c_1 + c_2) \ln \frac{c_1 + c_2}{\phi_1 + \phi_2} \le c_1 \ln \frac{c_1}{\phi_1} + c_2 \ln \frac{c_2}{\phi_2} .$$

To prove it, let $\zeta(y) = y \ln y$, with $\zeta(0) = 0$. It is convex on $y > 0$ because $\zeta''(y) = 1/y > 0$ there, and since it is continuous on $y \ge 0$ the convexity inequality extends to $y = 0$ by taking limits. Set $\omega = \phi_1/(\phi_1 + \phi_2)$, so that $1 - \omega = \phi_2/(\phi_1+\phi_2)$ and

$$\frac{c_1 + c_2}{\phi_1 + \phi_2} = \omega \frac{c_1}{\phi_1} + (1-\omega)\frac{c_2}{\phi_2} .$$

Convexity gives $\zeta$ of this point at most $\omega\, \zeta(c_1/\phi_1) + (1-\omega)\, \zeta(c_2/\phi_2)$. Multiplying through by $\phi_1 + \phi_2$,

$$(\phi_1 + \phi_2)\, \zeta\!\left(\frac{c_1 + c_2}{\phi_1 + \phi_2}\right) \le \phi_1\, \zeta\!\left(\frac{c_1}{\phi_1}\right) + \phi_2\, \zeta\!\left(\frac{c_2}{\phi_2}\right),$$

and writing out $\zeta$ gives the log-sum inequality. Now take $0 < \alpha < 1$ and assume $q(o) > 0$ for every $o$, which holds for the leopard model because no row of $\mathbf{A}$ is zero. Apply the inequality for each observation $o$ with $c_1 = (1-\alpha)p(o)$, $c_2 = \alpha \bar p(o)$, $\phi_1 = (1-\alpha)q(o)$ and $\phi_2 = \alpha \bar p(o)$. The left side is

$$\big[(1-\alpha)p(o) + \alpha \bar p(o)\big] \ln \frac{(1-\alpha)p(o) + \alpha \bar p(o)}{(1-\alpha)q(o) + \alpha \bar p(o)} ,$$

and summing over $o$ turns it into the divergence between the two mixtures. On the right, the factors $(1-\alpha)$ and $\alpha$ cancel inside each logarithm, leaving $(1-\alpha)\sum_o p(o)\ln[p(o)/q(o)] + \alpha \sum_o \bar p(o) \ln 1$. That is the inequality required. At $\alpha = 0$ or $\alpha = 1$ one of the terms is absent and the result holds with equality.

What has been proved is the chord bound along the segment from $\mathbf{A}$ to the uniform channel. Replacing $\bar p$ by the columns of a second arbitrary channel, the same argument shows that mutual information is convex in the channel for a fixed prior, but the problem needs only the special case. The widget's information-gain readout is a single observation's KL divergence, not this average, and is not covered by the result.

Exercise 1.6.8Explaining away with three causes

Extend the noisy-OR example of Lesson 4 to three independent causes, each with prior $0.1$ and each firing the observation with probability $0.9$, keeping the leak at $0.01$. Compute $P(s_1 = 1 \mid o = 1)$ and compare it with the two-cause answer of $0.505$. Explain the direction of the change.

Solution

There are eight joint states. Write $k$ for the number of causes present. The likelihood depends only on $k$:

$$P(o=1 \mid k) = 1 - (0.1)^k (0.99),$$

giving $0.01, 0.901, 0.9901, 0.99901$ for $k = 0,1,2,3$. The prior for a state with $k$ causes present is $0.1^k \, 0.9^{3-k}$, and there are $\binom{3}{k}$ such states. So

$$P(o=1) = \sum_{k=0}^{3} \binom{3}{k} 0.1^k 0.9^{3-k} \left[1 - 0.99 \times 0.1^k\right] = 0.25396 .$$

For the marginal, sum the unnormalised mass over the four states with $s_1 = 1$:

$$P(s_1=1, o=1) = 0.1 \sum_{k'=0}^{2} \binom{2}{k'} 0.1^{k'} 0.9^{2-k'} \left[1 - 0.99 \times 0.1^{\,k'+1}\right] = 0.09180 ,$$

so $P(s_1 = 1 \mid o = 1) = 0.09180 / 0.25396 = 0.3615$.

The answer falls from $0.505$ to $0.361$. With more candidate explanations available, the evidence is spread more thinly across them: the observation still says "something did this", but there are now three somethings to choose from and each individually gets less of the credit. This comparison concerns these two specified models. It does not establish a general monotonic relation between cause count and approximation error.

Exercise 1.6.9The survival curve †

Consider the homeostasis model from Lesson 1 with the action strength set to zero, $\kappa = 0$. It is then a two-dimensional random walk with independent increments of standard deviation $\sigma$ per step in each coordinate, starting at the origin, absorbed when $\|x\| > 1$.

(a) Use a small-step diffusion approximation to motivate an expected absorption time proportional to $\sigma^{-2}$. Why need this not be exact for a discrete walk?

(b) The notebook measures this by simulation. Predict the ratio of survival times at $\sigma = 0.09$ and $\sigma = 0.035$ before you run it.

Solution

(a) After $n$ steps the walk's displacement has variance $n\sigma^2$ in each coordinate, so the typical distance from the origin is of order $\sigma\sqrt{n}$. Absorption happens when this reaches the boundary at radius $1$, so $\sigma\sqrt{n} \sim 1$ and $n \sim \sigma^{-2}$. This estimates the scaling, not an exact discrete first-passage time. A finite step can overshoot the boundary; the approximation improves as the step size becomes small relative to the radius.

(b) The ratio is $(0.09 / 0.035)^{-2} = (0.035/0.09)^2 = 0.151$. Halving the noise should quadruple the lifetime, and the roughly 2.6-fold increase in $\sigma$ here should cut it by a factor of about $6.6$.

For stable regulation the unabsorbed process has a stationary variance, but an absorbing boundary can still be crossed. Compare survival distributions at fixed observation horizons rather than inferring indefinite survival from bounded variance.

Exercise 1.6.10The regulated walk

Take the homeostat $x_{t+1} = (1-\kappa)x_t + \sigma\xi_t$ of Lesson 1 with $0 < \kappa < 2$ and no absorbing boundary, starting from $x_0 = 0$. Write $v_t$ for the variance of one coordinate of $x_t$, and $v = \sigma^2/[\kappa(2-\kappa)]$ for its stationary variance.

(a) Solve the recursion $v_{t+1} = (1-\kappa)^2 v_t + \sigma^2$ from Lesson 1 exactly, and show that at fixed $t$ the result tends to $t\sigma^2$ as $\kappa \to 0$.

(b) At the lesson's gain $\kappa = 0.15$, after how many steps does $v_t$ first reach 95% of $v$?

(c) Show that the covariance of one coordinate at times $t$ and $t + j$ is $(1-\kappa)^j v_t$ for every whole number $j \ge 0$, so that it tends to $(1-\kappa)^j v$ once $t$ is large. What does a gain above one do to it?

(d) For large $t$, when $v_t$ has settled at $v$, find the probability that $x_t$ lies outside the unit circle. Evaluate it at $\sigma = 0.035$ and $\kappa = 0.15$, and say what it does and does not tell you about survival.

Solution

(a) Measure the variance from its stationary value, $v_t = v + \delta_t$. Substituting into the recursion,

$$v + \delta_{t+1} = (1-\kappa)^2 (v + \delta_t) + \sigma^2 .$$

The stationary value satisfies $v = (1-\kappa)^2 v + \sigma^2$, and subtracting that equation leaves $\delta_{t+1} = (1-\kappa)^2 \delta_t$. So $\delta_t = (1-\kappa)^{2t} \delta_0$, with $\delta_0 = v_0 - v = -v$ because the walk starts at the origin with no spread. Hence

$$v_t = v\left[1 - (1-\kappa)^{2t}\right] = \frac{\sigma^2\left[1 - (1-\kappa)^{2t}\right]}{\kappa(2-\kappa)} .$$

For the limit, expand the numerator by the binomial theorem, $(1-\kappa)^{2t} = 1 - 2t\kappa + O(\kappa^2)$, so $1 - (1-\kappa)^{2t} = 2t\kappa + O(\kappa^2)$. The denominator is $2\kappa - \kappa^2$. Dividing top and bottom by $\kappa$,

$$v_t = \sigma^2\,\frac{2t + O(\kappa)}{2 - \kappa} \;\longrightarrow\; t\sigma^2 \quad\text{as } \kappa \to 0 ,$$

which is the variance of the unregulated walk in Lesson 1. As a check at a small gain, $\kappa = 0.01$ and $t = 10$ give $v_t/\sigma^2 = 9.15$ against $t = 10$. The limit is taken at fixed $t$. For any fixed $\kappa > 0$ the factor $(1-\kappa)^{2t}$ eventually becomes negligible, and the variance stops growing and settles at $v$.

(b) From (a), $v_t \ge 0.95\,v$ exactly when $(1-\kappa)^{2t} \le 0.05$. Taking logarithms, and noting that $\ln(1-\kappa) < 0$ reverses the inequality on division,

$$t \ge \frac{\ln 0.05}{2\ln(1-\kappa)} = 9.22 ,$$

so the first such step is $t = 10$: after that many steps the variance is within 5% of its stationary value. The next problem relies on this when it discards the start of a simulated run.

(c) Applying the homeostat recursion $j$ times, by induction on $j$,

$$x_{t+j} = (1-\kappa)^j x_t + \sigma \sum_{i=0}^{j-1} (1-\kappa)^{j-1-i}\,\xi_{t+i} .$$

The case $j = 1$ is the recursion itself, and substituting the case $j$ into $x_{t+j+1} = (1-\kappa)x_{t+j} + \sigma\xi_{t+j}$ gives the case $j+1$. Now $x_t$ depends only on $\xi_0, \dots, \xi_{t-1}$, so it is independent of every $\xi_{t+i}$ with $i \ge 0$, and all of these have mean zero. Multiply one coordinate of each side by the same coordinate of $x_t$ and take expectations. Each noise term gives $\mathbb{E}[x_t \xi_{t+i}] = \mathbb{E}[x_t]\,\mathbb{E}[\xi_{t+i}] = 0$ by independence, so

$$\mathrm{Cov}(x_t, x_{t+j}) = (1-\kappa)^j\,\mathbb{E}[x_t^2] = (1-\kappa)^j v_t ,$$

using that every $x_t$ has mean zero. As $t$ grows this tends to $(1-\kappa)^j v$, and the correlation between the two times tends to $(1-\kappa)^j$. For $1 < \kappa < 2$ the base $1-\kappa$ is negative, so the correlation alternates in sign with the lag. Each step overcorrects and the displacement changes side. At lags 1, 2 and 3, a gain of $0.5$ gives correlations $0.5$, $0.25$ and $0.125$, and a gain of $1.5$ gives $-0.5$, $0.25$ and $-0.125$. Gains $\kappa$ and $2-\kappa$ give correlations of the same magnitude and opposite sign at odd lags, which is the difference between the two equally broad trajectories described in Lesson 1.

(d) Each coordinate of $x_t$ is, by repeated substitution, a fixed linear combination of the independent Gaussian variables $\xi_0, \dots, \xi_{t-1}$, and so is Gaussian, with mean zero and variance $v_t \to v$. The two coordinates are built from separate, independent noise and so are independent. Their joint density is $(2\pi v)^{-1}\exp[-\lVert x\rVert^2/(2v)]$, which depends on $x$ only through its distance from the origin. Write $R$ for that distance. In polar coordinates the area element is $R\,\mathrm{d}R$ times the element of angle, and integrating over the angle gives a factor $2\pi$, so

$$\begin{aligned} P(\lVert x_t\rVert > 1) &= \int_1^\infty \frac{R}{v}\, e^{-R^2/(2v)}\,\mathrm{d}R = \Big[-e^{-R^2/(2v)}\Big]_1^\infty \\ &= e^{-1/(2v)} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\kappa(2-\kappa)}{2\sigma^2}\right) . \end{aligned}$$

At the lesson's settings the exponent is $1/(2v) = 113.3$ and the probability is $6.45 \times 10^{-50}$. At the larger noise $\sigma = 0.09$ of the survival-curve problem, with the same gain, it is $3.64 \times 10^{-8}$.

The circle lies about 15 standard deviations from the origin in each coordinate, so this is a statement about the far tail of a Gaussian in an idealised model, and nobody could check a number like $6.45 \times 10^{-50}$ against a real body. It is the probability that the unabsorbed process is outside the circle at one late time. It is not the probability of having died by that time. Survival requires that the path never left, which is a statement about the whole path, and (c) shows successive positions are correlated, so the per-step probabilities cannot be multiplied as if they were independent trials. The waiting time needs the first-passage calculation mentioned in the warning in Lesson 1. What the formula does show is how the noise enters. Without feedback, the survival timescale of the previous problem varies as $\sigma^{-2}$. With feedback, the probability of being outside varies as $\exp[-\kappa(2-\kappa)/(2\sigma^2)]$, exponentially in $\sigma^{-2}$, so a change of noise by a factor of 2.6 moves it by 42 orders of magnitude.

Exercise 1.6.11An implementation

Work in a new cell of week01_numpy.ipynb, or in any Python session with NumPy.

(a) Write functions bayes(A, prior, o), returning the posterior and the evidence, entropy(p), kl(q, p) and mutual_information(A, prior). Make bayes refuse a likelihood matrix whose columns do not sum to one. Check your functions against the tawny-flash posterior of Lesson 3, its information gain $0.940$ nats, and the mutual information of the leopard model computed by both forms in the problems above. Then confirm that the refusal fires on the leopard matrix with its rows normalised instead of its columns, the mistake of the normalisation problem.

(b) Simulate the unabsorbed homeostat at $\sigma = 0.035$ and $\kappa = 0.15$ for 50,000 steps from the origin, discard the first 100, and estimate the stationary variance per coordinate, pooling the two coordinates, and the correlation between successive steps. Compare them with the regulated-walk problem. Repeat over 20 seeds. How large is the spread between seeds, and is the difference between your average and the formula within it?

Solution

(a) One implementation:

import numpy as np

def bayes(A, prior, o):
    if not np.allclose(A.sum(axis=0), 1):
        raise ValueError("columns of A must sum to one")
    joint = A[o] * prior
    evidence = joint.sum()
    return joint / evidence, evidence

def entropy(p):
    p = p[p > 0]
    return -np.sum(p * np.log(p))

def kl(q, p):
    keep = q > 0
    return np.sum(q[keep] * np.log(q[keep] / p[keep]))

def mutual_information(A, prior):
    evidence = A @ prior
    return sum(evidence[o] * kl(bayes(A, prior, o)[0], prior)
               for o in range(A.shape[0]))

A = np.array([[0.70, 0.15, 0.02],
              [0.25, 0.75, 0.08],
              [0.05, 0.10, 0.90]])
prior = np.array([0.08, 0.22, 0.70])

post, ev = bayes(A, prior, 0)
print(post, ev, kl(post, prior))

state_side = mutual_information(A, prior)
obs_side = entropy(A @ prior) - sum(prior[s] * entropy(A[:, s]) for s in range(3))
assert abs(state_side - obs_side) < 1e-12

A_rows = A / A.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
try:
    bayes(A_rows, prior, 0)
except ValueError as err:
    print("refused:", err)

The posterior is $(0.5437, 0.3204, 0.1359)$ with evidence $0.103$ and information gain $0.940$ nats, and both forms of the mutual information give $0.3706$ nats. The refusal fires on A_rows. The guard is necessary but not sufficient: a matrix with a negative entry and unit column sums would pass it. Without it, bayes would return a normalised posterior from the wrong matrix, which is the failure the normalisation problem describes, where the division by the evidence hides the error.

(b) Draw all the noise for a run at once, then apply the homeostat recursion step by step:

def run(seed, kappa=0.15, sigma=0.035, T=50_000):
    xi = np.random.default_rng(seed).standard_normal((T, 2))
    x = np.zeros(2)
    path = np.empty((T, 2))
    for t in range(T):
        x = (1 - kappa) * x + sigma * xi[t]
        path[t] = x
    return path

v = 0.035**2 / (0.15 * (2 - 0.15))
variances, correlations = [], []
for seed in range(20):
    tail = run(seed)[100:]
    var = np.mean(tail**2)
    variances.append(var)
    correlations.append(np.mean(tail[1:] * tail[:-1]) / var)

print(v, np.mean(variances), np.std(variances, ddof=1))
print(np.mean(correlations), np.std(correlations, ddof=1))

The variance is estimated as the mean square, pooled over both coordinates and all steps kept, because the mean is known to be zero. Discarding 100 steps is 11 times the settling time found in the regulated-walk problem; by then the variance deficit $(1-\kappa)^{2t}$ is negligible, so the start contributes no visible bias.

Seed 0 gives a variance of $0.004473$ against the formula's $v = 0.004414$, and a successive-step correlation of $0.8521$ against $1 - \kappa = 0.85$. Over 20 seeds the variance estimates have mean $0.004403$ and standard deviation $5.93 \times 10^{-5}$, which is 1.3% of $v$. The mean of 20 independent estimates has a standard error smaller by $\sqrt{ 20 }$, about 0.3% of $v$, and the mean lies 0.25% below $v$, within one standard error. The correlations have mean $0.8497$ and standard deviation $0.0021$.

Each run holds 99,800 numbers, yet one run's variance is uncertain at the level of 1.3%. If the $N = 99,800$ squares were independent, each would have variance $\mathbb{E}[x^4] - v^2 = 3v^2 - v^2 = 2v^2$ (the fourth moment of a zero-mean Gaussian is $3v^2$), and their mean would have relative standard deviation $\sqrt{2/N} = 0.45\%$, smaller than what is observed by a factor of 3.0.

The squares are not independent. Write $\rho = 1 - \kappa$ for the correlation between successive positions found in (c) of the regulated-walk problem. For two zero-mean jointly Gaussian variables $X$ and $Y$, $\mathrm{Cov}(X^2, Y^2) = 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)^2$. To see it, write $Y = cX + W$ with $c = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ and $W$ Gaussian and independent of $X$. Then $\mathbb{E}[X^2Y^2] = c^2\,\mathbb{E}[X^4] + \mathbb{E}[X^2]\,\mathbb{E}[W^2]$, the cross term vanishing because $\mathbb{E}[W] = 0$, while $\mathbb{E}[X^2]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2](c^2\,\mathbb{E}[X^2] + \mathbb{E}[W^2])$. Subtracting, and using $\mathbb{E}[X^4] = 3\,\mathbb{E}[X^2]^2$, leaves $2c^2\,\mathbb{E}[X^2]^2 = 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)^2$. So squares $j$ steps apart have covariance $2v^2\rho^{2j}$. The variance of a mean of $N$ terms is $N^{-2}$ times the sum of all their covariances, which for large $N$ is close to $N^{-1}$ times the sum over every lag, positive and negative:

$$\frac{2v^2}{N}\Big(1 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\rho^{2j}\Big) = \frac{2v^2}{N}\Big(1 + \frac{2\rho^2}{1-\rho^2}\Big) = \frac{2v^2}{N}\cdot\frac{1+\rho^2}{1-\rho^2} .$$

At $\rho = 0.85$ the factor $(1+\rho^2)/(1-\rho^2)$ is $6.21$, so the predicted relative spread is $0.45\% \times \sqrt{ 6.21 } = 1.12\%$. The observed 1.34% is 1.2 times that. A standard deviation estimated from 20 samples is itself uncertain by roughly $1/\sqrt{2(20-1)}$, about 16%, so the two agree within that uncertainty. The correlation between successive positions makes a run worth about $6.2$ times fewer independent samples than it has entries. A simulation that reports an average over correlated steps should report its spread over independent runs, as here, rather than a standard error that treats every step as independent.
