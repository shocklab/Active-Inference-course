Learning the model

What the mode leaves out

The rules of the last three lessons settle at the wrong values even when the model is exactly right. Why, by how much, and what expectation maximisation and hyperpriors change.

Week 3§460 min

Lesson 1 chose to climb the settled log joint $\hat\ell$ rather than the log evidence $\ln p(u; \omega)$, because the evidence needs an integral over the state. This lesson measures what that choice costs. The answer is a bias in every learned parameter, present even when the world is generated by exactly the model the animal holds, and in one case a collapse.

1The gradient of the evidence

DerivationThe Fisher identity

Write $p(u; \omega) = \int_0^\infty p(d, u; \omega)\,\mathrm dd$. Differentiate under the integral, then multiply and divide the integrand by $p(d, u; \omega)$:

$$\frac{\partial \ln p(u; \omega)}{\partial \omega} \;=\; \frac{1}{p(u; \omega)}\int_0^\infty \frac{\partial p(d, u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\,\mathrm dd \;=\; \int_0^\infty \frac{p(d, u; \omega)}{p(u; \omega)}\,\frac{\partial \ln p(d, u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\,\mathrm dd .$$

The ratio in front is the posterior $p(d \mid u; \omega)$, and $\ln p(d, u; \omega)$ is the log joint $\ell$. So

$$\frac{\partial \ln p(u; \omega)}{\partial \omega} \;=\; \mathbb E_{d\mid u}\!\left[\frac{\partial \ell(d, u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\right] ,$$
(3.4.1)

the average over the posterior $p(d \mid u; \omega)$ at the current parameters.

The slope of the log evidence is the posterior average of the same partial derivative the animal uses. The animal evaluates it at one point, the mode, where (3.4.1) averages it over the whole posterior.

At Week 2's observation the posterior has mean $2.0900$ and variance $0.5198$, against a mode of $1.5500$. The two versions of each derivative are:

Parameter At the mode Posterior average Slope of $\ln p(u)$, by finite difference
$d_p$ $-0.5052$ $0.0348$ $0.0348$
$\Sigma_p$ $-0.3435$ $-0.1808$ $-0.1808$
$\Sigma_u$ $-4.6490$ $-1.1518$ $-1.1518$

The last two columns agree, as (3.4.1) requires. The first does not: for $\Sigma_u$ it is several times too steep, and for $d_p$ it has the wrong sign. On this one sound the evidence would be raised by moving the prior location up, and the animal moves it down.

2Mean zero at the truth

One trial proves little. By Lesson 1, a rule is judged by where its drift vanishes and whether that zero attracts.

DerivationThe score has mean zero

Suppose the world draws $u$ from $p(u; \omega^{*})$, the model's own evidence at some true parameter $\omega^{*}$. The expected slope of the log evidence at the truth is

$$\int p(u; \omega^{*})\,\frac{\partial \ln p(u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\bigg|_{\omega^{*}}\mathrm du \;=\; \int \frac{\partial p(u; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\bigg|_{\omega^{*}}\mathrm du \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial \omega}\int p(u; \omega)\,\mathrm du\,\bigg|_{\omega^{*}} \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\,1 \;=\; 0 .$$

The slope of the log evidence is called the score, and this is the statement that its mean is zero at the true parameter. Combined with (3.4.1), it says that a rule which averages $\partial\ell/\partial\omega$ over the posterior, rather than reading it at the mode, has zero drift at the truth whenever the world is one of the model's own settings.

Definition 3.4.1Posterior-averaged rule and point rule

The posterior-averaged rule changes a parameter on trial $n$ by

$$\omega \;\leftarrow\; \omega \;+\; \beta\,\mathbb E_{d\mid u_n}\!\left[\frac{\partial \ell(d, u_n; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\right] .$$
(3.4.2)

The rule of Lesson 1 reads the same derivative at the mode instead, and from here on is called the point rule.

The animal does not have the posterior average; the simulations compute it on every trial by quadrature over the posterior, on 801 points, so that the two rules can be compared on the same trials. Nothing guarantees zero drift at the truth for the point rule.

DerivationThe posterior-averaged rule climbs towards the world

By (3.4.1) the drift of (3.4.2) is the process average of the score,

$$\varphi(\omega) \;=\; \mathbb E_{P^*}\!\left[\frac{\partial \ln p(u; \omega)}{\partial\omega}\right] \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\,\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\ln p(u; \omega)\big] .$$

Write $P^*(u)$ for the density of the observations the process produces. Adding and subtracting $\ln P^*(u)$ inside the average, as Week 1, Lesson 2 did with surprise and entropy, with the sum there replaced by an integral,

$$\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\ln p(u;\omega)\big] \;=\; \mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\ln P^*(u)\big] \;-\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[P^*\,\|\,p(\cdot\,;\omega)\big] .$$

The first term does not involve $\omega$, so

$$\varphi(\omega) \;=\; -\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[P^*\,\|\,p(\cdot\,;\omega)\big] .$$

The drift is minus the slope, in $\omega$, of the divergence of the world's distribution of sounds relative to the model's. When the world is one of the model's settings, that divergence is zero at the truth and positive elsewhere, so the truth is a minimum of it: the drift vanishes there, and where the divergence curves upwards, $\varphi' < 0$ and the truth attracts. The score argument above is the first half of this; the second half is why the posterior-averaged rule returns to the truth after a disturbance.

3The linear case, exactly

In a linear Gaussian model every expectation can be done by hand, so the size of the bias can be computed exactly. Let the world draw $d$ from $\mathcal N(d_p, \Sigma_p^{*})$ on the whole line, and let $u$ be $d$ plus noise of variance $\Sigma_u$, so that $u$ has mean $d_p$ and variance $\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u$. The star marks the process, as in Lesson 1. The animal knows $d_p$ and $\Sigma_u$ and learns its prior variance $\Sigma_p$, with no truncation.

DerivationThe stationary points of the point rule

With prior variance $\Sigma_p$, the mode is the precision-weighted average of Week 2, Lesson 4,

$$\hat d - d_p \;=\; \frac{\Sigma_p}{\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u}\,(u - d_p) .$$

The point rule's update for the prior variance is the derivative of Lesson 2 without the truncation term, $\tfrac12\big((\hat d - d_p)^2/\Sigma_p^2 - 1/\Sigma_p\big)$. Its average over the world uses $\mathbb E_{P^*}[(u - d_p)^2] = \Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u$:

$$\mathbb E_{P^*}\!\left[\frac{(\hat d - d_p)^2}{\Sigma_p^2}\right] - \frac{1}{\Sigma_p} \;=\; \frac{\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u}{(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2} - \frac{1}{\Sigma_p} .$$

This vanishes where $\Sigma_p(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u) = (\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2$. Expanded,

$$\Sigma_p^2 + (\Sigma_u - \Sigma_p^{*})\,\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u^2 \;=\; 0 , \qquad \Sigma_p \;=\; \tfrac12\Big[(\Sigma_p^{*} - \Sigma_u) \pm \sqrt{(\Sigma_p^{*} - \Sigma_u)^2 - 4\Sigma_u^2}\,\Big] .$$
(3.4.3)

Real positive roots need $\Sigma_p^{*} - \Sigma_u \geq 2\Sigma_u$, that is $\Sigma_p^{*} \geq 3\Sigma_u$. Neither root is the truth unless $\Sigma_u = 0$: substituting $\Sigma_p = \Sigma_p^{*}$ into the quadratic leaves $\Sigma_p^{*}\Sigma_u + \Sigma_u^2$, which is positive.

The sign of the drift settles which root attracts. Half the bracket above, put over the common denominator $2\Sigma_p(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2$, is

$$\varphi(\Sigma_p) \;=\; -\,\frac{\Sigma_p^2 + (\Sigma_u - \Sigma_p^{*})\,\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u^2}{2\Sigma_p(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2} .$$

The denominator is positive, so the drift has the opposite sign to the quadratic in (3.4.3). That quadratic is $\Sigma_u^2 > 0$ at $\Sigma_p = 0$ and positive for large $\Sigma_p$, and its discriminant factorises as $(\Sigma_p^{*} - \Sigma_u)^2 - 4\Sigma_u^2 = (\Sigma_p^{*} - 3\Sigma_u)(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u)$. Three cases follow.

  • $\Sigma_p^{*} < 3\Sigma_u$: no real root, the quadratic is positive throughout, the drift is negative for every $\Sigma_p$, and the learned variance falls towards zero.
  • $\Sigma_p^{*} = 3\Sigma_u$: a double root at $\Sigma_p = \Sigma_u$, where the quadratic is $(\Sigma_p - \Sigma_u)^2$. The drift is negative on both sides and zero only there, so for the mean dynamics, which follow the drift, the root attracts from above and repels from below. A learner with a fixed rate does not follow the drift exactly. Its fluctuations can carry it below the root, and there the drift points down, but the observations are Gaussian and so unbounded, and one large enough observation gives an upward step that can take the variance back above the root. Whether the learner eventually collapses is a question about its sample paths that the drift alone does not answer.
  • $\Sigma_p^{*} > 3\Sigma_u$: two roots. The drift is negative below the smaller, positive between them and negative above the larger. The smaller root repels and separates collapse from survival; the larger attracts, and is where the point rule settles.

Why the drift turns negative near zero can be read off the mode. The shrinkage factor $\Sigma_p/(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$ in the mode depends on the variance being learned. A smaller $\Sigma_p$ pulls each mode closer to $d_p$, so the modes' spread $\Sigma_p^2(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u)/(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2$ shrinks like $\Sigma_p^2$ while the target it is compared with, $\Sigma_p$, shrinks only like $\Sigma_p$. Below the smaller root the modes look less spread than the prior claims, the rule narrows the prior, and the narrower prior pulls the modes in further. The rule feeds back on itself.

DerivationThe posterior-averaged rule in the linear case

The posterior variance is $\Sigma_{\text{post}} = \Sigma_p\Sigma_u/(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$, the reciprocal of the summed precisions, as derived in Week 2, Lesson 4. The posterior average of $(d - d_p)^2$ is $(\hat d - d_p)^2 + \Sigma_{\text{post}}$. The drift vanishes where

$$\frac{\Sigma_p^2(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u)}{(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2} + \frac{\Sigma_p\Sigma_u}{\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u} \;=\; \Sigma_p .$$

Multiply by $(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2/\Sigma_p$: $\Sigma_p(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u) + \Sigma_u(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u) = (\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2$. Expanding both sides and cancelling $\Sigma_u^2$ and $2\Sigma_p\Sigma_u$ leaves $\Sigma_p\Sigma_p^{*} = \Sigma_p^2$, whose positive solution is $\Sigma_p = \Sigma_p^{*}$.

With $\Sigma_p^{*} = 1$ and $\Sigma_u = 0.1$, the roots of (3.4.3) are $0.011252$ and $0.888748$; the larger differs from the truth by $-11.1$ per cent. A simulation in values.py with the log-variance step of Lesson 2, started at $\Sigma_p = 1$, averages $0.908$ under the point rule and $1.019$ under the posterior-averaged rule. With $\Sigma_u = 0.5$, so that $3\Sigma_u = 1.5$ exceeds $\Sigma_p^{*}$, the point rule takes the variance from $1$ to $4.5 \times 10^{-7}$ in $4000$ trials.

4The missing term

The point rule prefers a smaller variance because of what $\hat\ell$ is.

DerivationThe settled log joint against the evidence

The posterior is Gaussian, so the joint at any $d$ is the evidence times the posterior density there: $p(d, u) = p(u)\,\mathcal N(d; \hat d, \Sigma_{\text{post}})$. At the mode the density is $1/\sqrt{2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}}}$, so

$$\ln p(u; \Sigma_p) \;=\; \hat\ell(u; \Sigma_p) \;+\; \tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}}) .$$
(3.4.4)

Average over the world. Under the model, $u \sim \mathcal N(d_p, \Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$, and under the world $\mathbb E_{P^*}[(u - d_p)^2] = \Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u$, so

$$\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\ln p(u; \Sigma_p)\big] \;=\; -\frac{\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u}{2(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)} - \tfrac12\ln\big(2\pi(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)\big) .$$

This is largest where its derivative, $(\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u)/\big(2(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)^2\big) - 1/\big(2(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)\big)$, vanishes, which is at $\Sigma_p = \Sigma_p^{*}$. Subtracting $\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}})$, with $\ln\Sigma_{\text{post}} = \ln\Sigma_p + \ln\Sigma_u - \ln(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$, gives

$$\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\hat\ell(u; \Sigma_p)\big] \;=\; -\frac{\Sigma_p^{*} + \Sigma_u}{2(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)} - \tfrac12\ln\Sigma_p - \ln(2\pi) - \tfrac12\ln\Sigma_u .$$

Its derivative in $\Sigma_p$ is the point rule's drift computed above, as the envelope theorem of Lesson 1 says it must be.

The two differ by $\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}})$. As $\Sigma_p \to 0$ the evidence falls, because a prior with no spread cannot account for the spread in $u$. The settled log joint rises without bound, because the prior's density at its own peak grows like $1/\sqrt{\Sigma_p}$ and the mode moves to that peak. The term $\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}})$ is the log of the posterior's width, and adding it cancels the growth.

Week 4 adds $\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}})$ back. Its free energy under a Gaussian approximation to the posterior contains the log of the approximation's width. In this model, where the posterior is Gaussian, the best such approximation is exact, and then the free energy equals minus the log evidence: the negative free energy is the log evidence.

6Where the bias comes from

Both rules use the same per-trial derivative. Write it as a function of the state, $\psi(d) = \partial\ell(d, u; \omega)/\partial\omega$, for one trial and the current parameters. The point rule uses $\psi(\hat d)$; the posterior-averaged rule uses $\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[\psi(d)]$. Write $m = \mathbb E_{d\mid u}[d]$ for the posterior mean, $m$ being Week 2's placeholder for a Gaussian's mean put to the same use, and $V$ for the posterior variance.

DerivationThe gap between the two rules on one trial

Expand $\psi$ about the mode to second order,

$$\psi(d) \;\approx\; \psi(\hat d) + \psi'(\hat d)\,(d - \hat d) + \tfrac12 \psi''(\hat d)\,(d - \hat d)^2 ,$$

and average over the posterior. The first moment is $\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[d - \hat d] = m - \hat d$. The second is $\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[(d - \hat d)^2] = \mathbb E_{d\mid u}[(d - m + m - \hat d)^2] = V + (m - \hat d)^2$, the cross term vanishing because $d - m$ averages to zero. So

$$\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[\psi(d)] - \psi(\hat d) \;\approx\; \psi'(\hat d)\,(m - \hat d) \;+\; \tfrac12 \psi''(\hat d)\,\big[V + (m - \hat d)^2\big] ,$$
(3.4.5)

with equality when $\psi$ is a polynomial of degree at most two in $d$.

The posterior-averaged rule has zero drift at the truth, so the point rule's drift there is minus the process average of (3.4.5). Two features of the posterior drive it: the offset of the mode from the mean, which a skewed posterior has, and the posterior variance, which every posterior has. The derivatives of Lesson 2 and Lesson 3 say how each parameter responds.

  • Prior location. $\psi = (d - d_p)/\Sigma_p - \zeta_p$ is linear in $d$, so (3.4.5) is exact and equals $(m - \hat d)/\Sigma_p$. Only the offset matters. In the linear model of Lesson 1 the posterior is Gaussian, $m = \hat d$, and the location is learned without bias. In Week 2's model the link flattens with distance, the posterior has a long tail towards far sources, the mode lies below the mean, and the learned location is too small.
  • Prior variance. $\psi$ is quadratic in $d$ with $\psi'' = 1/\Sigma_p^2$, so (3.4.5) is exact. Since $\psi$ is $(d - d_p)^2/(2\Sigma_p^2)$ plus terms that do not depend on $d$, the same gap can also be written directly as $\big[\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[(d - d_p)^2] - (\hat d - d_p)^2\big]/(2\Sigma_p^2)$, and $\mathbb E_{d\mid u}[(d - d_p)^2] = V + (m - d_p)^2$ by the same cross-term argument, so the bracket is $V + (m - d_p)^2 - (\hat d - d_p)^2$. The first term is positive on every trial: the modes leave out each posterior's own variance. The other two compare the mean's squared deviation with the mode's, and either can be the larger. At $u = 1$ under Week 2's model the mode's squared deviation from $d_p$ is $0.9458$ and the posterior average of $(d - d_p)^2$ is only $0.6839$, so on that trial the point rule's squared deviation is the larger. Averaged over the process, the law of total variance splits the posterior-averaged term into $\mathbb E_{P^*}[V]$ and the spread of the posterior means $m$ about $d_p$; the point rule has neither, only the spread of the modes. The quadrature over the world gives the net effect: the learned variance comes out too small.
  • Sensory variance. $\psi$ depends on $d$ through $\delta_u^2/(2\Sigma_u^2)$, and $\mathrm d^2\delta_u^2/\mathrm dd^2 = 2g'(d)^2 - 2\delta_u g''(d)$. This $\psi$ is not a polynomial in $d$, so (3.4.5) is an approximation. Where the residual at the mode is small, $\psi'' > 0$ and the variance term has the same sign as for the prior. The quadrature in the table confirms the sign: the learned sensory variance is too small.
  • Source power. $\psi = \varepsilon_u h(d)$, and at a small residual $\psi' > 0$ while $\psi'' < 0$ (an exercise asks for both). This $\psi$ is not a polynomial either, and the two terms of (3.4.5) pull in opposite directions. The full calculation puts the learned power $12.0$ per cent from the truth in the world equal to Week 2's model, an overestimate, and $-11.3$ per cent from it in the louder world of Lesson 3, an underestimate.

The second-order formula does not settle the power's sign. Averaged over the louder world, at the true power, the offset term of (3.4.5) is $0.7702$ and the variance term $-1.5131$. Their sum, $-0.7429$, is negative, while the exact average of the gap, computed from the whole posterior, is $0.0303$, positive; the point rule's drift at the truth is minus that, and the power is underestimated. The terms the expansion drops reverse the sign. In the world equal to Week 2's model the sum is $-2.7267$ against an exact $-0.0385$: the signs agree and the sizes do not. Where $\psi$ is a polynomial of degree at most two, as for the prior's location and variance, (3.4.5) is exact and names the two contributions. Elsewhere it is a local approximation that names the same two contributions without fixing their net effect, and the signs and sizes in the table come from the full calculation over the world.

DerivationWhat the settled log joint adds to the evidence

For any model and any $u$, factor the joint at its maximum: $p(u) = \int_0^\infty p(d, u)\,\mathrm dd = e^{\hat\ell}\int_0^\infty e^{\ell(d) - \hat\ell}\,\mathrm dd$. Since $p(\hat d \mid u) = e^{\hat\ell}/p(u)$,

$$\hat\ell(u; \omega) \;=\; \ln p(u; \omega) \;+\; \ln p(\hat d \mid u; \omega) .$$
(3.4.6)

The settled log joint is the log evidence plus the log of the posterior's height at its peak. In the linear model that height is $1/\sqrt{2\pi\Sigma_{\text{post}}}$, which is (3.4.4) again. Climbing $\hat\ell$ climbs the evidence and, at the same time, rewards any parameter change that makes the posterior taller and narrower, whether or not it explains the sounds better. That is the general reason the point rule shrinks variances, and the reason it can run away to zero when nothing else holds them up.

CarefulThe bias comes from the states

The rules are biased because the state is replaced by its mode before the parameter update is computed. The same rules, applied to the posterior average of the state's contribution, have zero drift at the truth. A good parameter learner on top of a point estimate of the state is not enough. The mode leaves out the posterior's spread, and where the posterior is asymmetric it is also not the posterior mean; how much each omission costs depends on how the parameter's derivative depends on the state. Of the four parameters computed here, the sensory variance is off by the most.

CarefulPoint estimates of parameters

The animal also holds each parameter as a single number. That is a separate simplification, and it costs something different: the animal has no measure of how well it knows a parameter, so a value learned from ten sounds and one learned from ten thousand are used with the same confidence. With many trials the uncertainty about a parameter shrinks, and holding only its best value loses little. Early in learning, or after the world changes, it loses a great deal. Week 11 keeps counts for the parameters of discrete models, which is the simplest way to carry that uncertainty.

7Expectation maximisation

The posterior-averaged rule is a stochastic, online form of an algorithm statisticians use to fit models with unobserved variables. Expectation maximisation, or EM, alternates two steps from the current parameter $\omega_{\text{old}}$:

  • the E step computes the posterior $p(d \mid u; \omega_{\text{old}})$;
  • the M step chooses the new parameter to maximise $\mathbb E_{p(d \mid u;\, \omega_{\text{old}})}\big[\ell(d, u; \omega)\big]$, the log joint averaged over that fixed posterior.

At $\omega = \omega_{\text{old}}$ the gradient of the M step's objective is the posterior average of $\partial\ell/\partial\omega$, which by (3.4.1) is the slope of the log evidence. EM is therefore an ascent method on the evidence; Week 4 proves that each full EM iteration never lowers it.

The animal's scheme departs from EM in three ways:

  1. it replaces the posterior by its mode in the E step;
  2. it takes one small gradient step in the M step instead of maximising;
  3. it does both one trial at a time.

The second and third change the speed and the noise of learning but not where the drift vanishes: by Lesson 1, a run with a small fixed rate fluctuates about that zero by order $\sqrt\beta$, with a stationary mean that the drift's curvature can displace by order $\beta$. The first changes where the drift vanishes, and that is the bias computed above. Buckley et al. (2017), in their introduction, trace the free energy back to work by Hinton and colleagues in which a function like it implements a variant of EM.

8Hyperpriors

Lesson 2 noted that the original model forbade small variances outright. A smooth alternative is to give the variance a prior of its own. A prior over a parameter of a prior or likelihood is a hyperprior.

DerivationA hyperprior as imaginary trials

Take the density on $\Sigma > 0$

$$p(\Sigma) \;\propto\; \Sigma^{-(n_0/2 + 1)}\exp\!\left(-\frac{n_0\Sigma_0}{2\Sigma}\right),$$

an inverse-gamma density with parameters written so that $n_0 > 0$ and $\Sigma_0 > 0$ can be read in a moment. Suppose $N$ trials give residuals $\delta_n$ whose log likelihood in $\Sigma$ is $\sum_n\big[-\delta_n^2/(2\Sigma) - \tfrac12\ln\Sigma\big]$, up to a constant. Adding the log hyperprior,

$$-\frac{\sum_n \delta_n^2 + n_0\Sigma_0}{2\Sigma} \;-\; \frac{N + n_0 + 2}{2}\,\ln\Sigma .$$

The hyperprior has the form of $n_0$ extra trials, each with squared residual $\Sigma_0$, plus a constant $2$. Setting the derivative in $\Sigma$ to zero,

$$\frac{\sum_n \delta_n^2 + n_0\Sigma_0}{2\Sigma^2} \;=\; \frac{N + n_0 + 2}{2\Sigma} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Sigma \;=\; \frac{\sum_n \delta_n^2 + n_0\Sigma_0}{N + n_0 + 2} .$$
(3.4.7)

So $n_0$ says how many trials' worth of confidence the hyperprior carries, and $\Sigma_0$ is its guess at the variance. Without data the optimum is $n_0\Sigma_0/(n_0 + 2)$; with many trials the data dominate. Even if every residual is zero, (3.4.7) gives a positive variance, and the objective is bounded above, since as $\Sigma \to 0$ the term $-n_0\Sigma_0/(2\Sigma)$ falls to $-\infty$ faster than $-\ln\Sigma$ rises. This removes the unbounded direction found in Lesson 1's warning and in the figure above.

An online learner has no fixed $N$: it goes on learning. To use the hyperprior online, one has to choose how many trials it is shared across, and that choice is part of the model, not something the derivation supplies. Shared evenly over a window of $N$ trials, the hyperprior adds $\tfrac1N\big[n_0\Sigma_0/(2\Sigma) - (n_0/2 + 1)\big]$ to each trial's log-variance step, the derivative of $\tfrac1N\ln p(\Sigma)$ with respect to $\ln\Sigma$. As $\Sigma \to 0$ that term grows without bound and pushes the variance back up, which is the smooth version of a floor. A hard floor is the limiting case of a hyperprior with zero density below the floor and no preference above it.

9Learning the precision of the ear

A precision decides how far the estimate follows each channel.

DerivationThe ear's share

Replace the link near the prior location by its tangent, $g(d) \approx g(d_p) + g'(d_p)(d - d_p)$. The log joint is then quadratic in $d$, and setting its derivative to zero,

$$-\Pi_p(d - d_p) + \Pi_u g'(d_p)\big[u - g(d_p) - g'(d_p)(d - d_p)\big] \;=\; 0 ,$$

gives

$$\hat d - d_p \;=\; \frac{\Pi_u g'(d_p)}{\Pi_p + \Pi_u g'(d_p)^2}\,\big(u - g(d_p)\big) .$$

Multiply by $g'(d_p)$. The left side becomes the change in the predicted intensity, to the same order, and

$$g'(d_p)\,(\hat d - d_p) \;=\; \frac{\Pi_u g'(d_p)^2}{\Pi_p + \Pi_u g'(d_p)^2}\,\big(u - g(d_p)\big) .$$
(3.4.8)

The prediction moves the fraction in (3.4.8) of the way from what the prior predicts to what was heard. Its denominator is the Gauss-Newton curvature of Week 2, Lesson 4 at the prior location, so the fraction is also the ear's part of the posterior precision. Call it the ear's share. With Week 2's values it is $0.385$. Learning $\Sigma_u$ is learning that number, which is how much to trust the ear. Week 8 develops this into attention, as the optimisation of precisions.

In the widget the world's sensory variance is $\Sigma_u^{*} = 0.3$, three times what the animal believes. The animal learns $\Sigma_u$ alone with $\beta = 0.01$, under either rule, on the same sequence of trials.

The posterior-averaged rule reaches $0.3000$ by the last trial and averages $0.2933$ over the second half. The ear's share should fall from $0.385$ to $0.172$, and at that run's average it is $0.176$.

The point rule's drift, computed by quadrature over the world, vanishes at $\Sigma_u = 0.1017$. That is just above the $0.1$ the animal started with: the drift there is $0.00708$ in $\ln\Sigma_u$, positive and small. At the zero the ear's share is $0.381$, against $0.385$ at the start. A channel three times noisier than the animal believes leaves its trust in the ear almost where it was. The run in the widget averages $0.0957$ over its second half, slightly below the start, with a share of $0.395$; one run with a fixed rate fluctuates about the zero of the drift, as Lesson 1 showed, and this one happens to sit below it.

The reason is the one the linear case showed. The point rule measures residuals at the mode, and the mode is the state that makes the residual small, subject to the prior. Part of each trial's noise is absorbed by moving the estimate, so the residuals the rule sees are smaller than the noise, and the variance learned from them is too small. The point rule does learn a larger variance in the noisier world, $0.1017$ against $0.0266$ when the world is Week 2's model, but both are far below the truth, and in the noisier world the learned value barely moves from where the animal started.
