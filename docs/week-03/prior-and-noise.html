Learning the prior and the noise

The derivatives of the log joint with respect to the prior location and the two variances, what each update does, and what happens to a variance near zero.

Week 3§250 min

Lesson 1 reduced learning to one quantity per parameter: the partial derivative of the log joint, evaluated at the settled state. This lesson computes it for the prior location $d_p$, the prior variance $\Sigma_p$ and the sensory variance $\Sigma_u$, starting from the full log joint Lesson 1 restored,

$$\ell \;=\; -\frac{(d - d_p)^2}{2\Sigma_p} \;-\; \tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_p) \;-\; \ln Z_p \;-\; \frac{\big(u - g(d)\big)^2}{2\Sigma_u} \;-\; \tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_u), \qquad Z_p = \int_0^\infty \mathcal N(x; d_p, \Sigma_p)\,\mathrm dx .$$
(3.2.1)

The source power $\theta$ sits inside $g$ and is the subject of Lesson 3. The prediction errors are Week 2's,

$$\varepsilon_p \;=\; \frac{d_p - d}{\Sigma_p}, \qquad \varepsilon_u \;=\; \frac{u - g(d)}{\Sigma_u},$$

and the precisions are $\Pi_p = 1/\Sigma_p$ and $\Pi_u = 1/\Sigma_u$.

1The normaliser of the truncated prior

$Z_p$ is the one term Week 2 could ignore entirely, because it contains neither $d$ nor $u$. It does contain both prior parameters, so its derivatives are needed first.

DerivationThe derivative of the normaliser in the location

Write $N(x) = \mathcal N(x; d_p, \Sigma_p)$ for short. Differentiating under the integral sign, which is allowed because the integrand and its derivative in $d_p$ are continuous and bounded by an integrable Gaussian on the half-line,

$$\frac{\partial Z_p}{\partial d_p} \;=\; \int_0^\infty \frac{\partial N(x)}{\partial d_p}\,\mathrm dx .$$

The Gaussian depends on $x$ and $d_p$ only through $x - d_p$, so moving $d_p$ up is the same as moving $x$ down:

$$\frac{\partial N}{\partial d_p} \;=\; \frac{x - d_p}{\Sigma_p}\,N(x) \;=\; -\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} .$$

The integral of a derivative is a difference of end values, and $N(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$:

$$\frac{\partial Z_p}{\partial d_p} \;=\; -\int_0^\infty \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\,\mathrm dx \;=\; -\big[N(x)\big]_0^\infty \;=\; N(0) \;=\; \mathcal N(0; d_p, \Sigma_p) .$$
(3.2.2)

The result says that moving the location up by a small amount moves a strip of mass of height $N(0)$ across the boundary at zero and into the half-line.

DerivationThe derivative of the normaliser in the variance

Differentiate the Gaussian density with respect to its variance. Its logarithm is $-(x - d_p)^2/(2\Sigma_p) - \tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_p)$, so

$$\frac{\partial N}{\partial \Sigma_p} \;=\; N(x)\left[\frac{(x - d_p)^2}{2\Sigma_p^2} - \frac{1}{2\Sigma_p}\right] .$$

Now differentiate it twice in $x$. Once gives $\partial N/\partial x = -\big[(x - d_p)/\Sigma_p\big]N$. Again, by the product rule,

$$\frac{\partial^2 N}{\partial x^2} \;=\; -\frac{1}{\Sigma_p}N \;+\; \frac{(x - d_p)^2}{\Sigma_p^2}N \;=\; 2\,\frac{\partial N}{\partial \Sigma_p} .$$

So $\partial N/\partial\Sigma_p = \tfrac12\,\partial^2 N/\partial x^2$, the heat equation with the variance in the role of time. Integrate over the half-line, and use $\partial N/\partial x \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$:

$$\frac{\partial Z_p}{\partial \Sigma_p} \;=\; \frac12\int_0^\infty \frac{\partial^2 N}{\partial x^2}\,\mathrm dx \;=\; \frac12\Big[\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\Big]_0^\infty \;=\; -\frac12\,\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x = 0} \;=\; -\frac{d_p}{2\Sigma_p}\,\mathcal N(0; d_p, \Sigma_p) .$$
(3.2.3)

Widening the prior spreads mass in both directions, and the half that crosses zero is lost, so $Z_p$ falls when $d_p > 0$. At Week 2's values $\mathcal N(0; d_p, \Sigma_p) = 0.053991$, and (3.2.3) gives $-0.053991$; a finite-difference derivative of $Z_p$ agrees to eight decimal places, and the check is an assertion in this week's values.py. Both derivatives enter through $\ln Z_p$, divided by $Z_p$, so it is convenient to name the ratio

$$\zeta_p \;=\; \frac{\mathcal N(0; d_p, \Sigma_p)}{Z_p},$$
(3.2.4)

which is $0.055248$ at Week 2's values. It is the height of the prior density at the boundary, as a fraction of the mass that lies inside it.

2The three derivatives

DerivationThe prior location

Only the first and third terms of (3.2.1) contain $d_p$. The first gives $(d - d_p)/\Sigma_p$ by the chain rule, exactly as the corresponding derivative in $d$ did in Week 2, Lesson 3 but with the opposite sign, because $d_p$ enters the square with a minus sign. The third gives $-(1/Z_p)\,\partial Z_p/\partial d_p = -\zeta_p$ by (3.2.2). So

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial d_p} \;=\; \frac{d - d_p}{\Sigma_p} - \zeta_p \;=\; -\varepsilon_p - \zeta_p .$$
(3.2.5)
DerivationThe prior variance

The first, second and third terms contain $\Sigma_p$. Differentiating $-(d - d_p)^2/(2\Sigma_p)$ gives $+(d - d_p)^2/(2\Sigma_p^2)$; differentiating $-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_p)$ gives $-1/(2\Sigma_p)$; and $-\ln Z_p$ gives $-(1/Z_p)\,\partial Z_p/\partial\Sigma_p = +d_p\zeta_p/(2\Sigma_p)$ by (3.2.3). Since $(d - d_p)^2/\Sigma_p^2 = \varepsilon_p^2$ and $1/\Sigma_p = \Pi_p$,

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \Sigma_p} \;=\; \tfrac12\big(\varepsilon_p^2 - \Pi_p\big) \;+\; \frac{d_p\,\zeta_p}{2\Sigma_p} .$$
(3.2.6)
DerivationThe sensory variance

Only the last two terms contain $\Sigma_u$, and they have the same form as the prior's first two without a normaliser to correct:

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \Sigma_u} \;=\; \frac{\big(u - g(d)\big)^2}{2\Sigma_u^2} - \frac{1}{2\Sigma_u} \;=\; \tfrac12\big(\varepsilon_u^2 - \Pi_u\big) .$$
(3.2.7)

At Week 2's observation and its mode, $\varepsilon_p = 0.4500$ and $\varepsilon_u = 0.8379$, so the three derivatives are $-0.5052$, $-0.3435$ and $-4.6490$. Each agrees with a finite-difference derivative of (3.2.1) at the same point.

3What each update does

The prior location moves opposite to the prior error, which means towards the inferred state: if the state was inferred below the prior location, $\varepsilon_p > 0$ and $d_p$ falls. The term $-\zeta_p$ adds a steady push downwards that does not depend on the trial. The drift of Lesson 1, the update averaged over the trials the process produces, vanishes where

$$\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\hat d\big] \;=\; d_p + \Sigma_p\zeta_p .$$
(3.2.8)

The right-hand side is the mean of the truncated prior.

DerivationThe mean of the truncated prior

With $N(x)$ as before, the mean is $\int_0^\infty x\,N(x)\,\mathrm dx/Z_p$. Write $x = d_p + (x - d_p)$ and use $(x - d_p)N = -\Sigma_p\,\partial N/\partial x$ from the derivation of (3.2.2):

$$\int_0^\infty x\,N\,\mathrm dx \;=\; d_p Z_p \;-\; \Sigma_p\int_0^\infty \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\,\mathrm dx \;=\; d_p Z_p + \Sigma_p N(0) .$$

Dividing by $Z_p$ gives $d_p + \Sigma_p\zeta_p$, which is $2.055248$ at Week 2's values; the same number by quadrature is asserted in values.py.

So the stationary condition reads: the average inferred distance equals the mean of the prior the animal holds. "The mean chases the inferred state" is the informal version, and the $\zeta_p$ term is what makes it the prior's actual mean rather than its location parameter.

The prior variance grows when (3.2.6) is positive. Multiplying it by $2\Sigma_p^2$, that is when

$$(d - d_p)^2 \;>\; \Sigma_p\big(1 - d_p\zeta_p\big):$$

the squared distance between the inferred state and the prior location exceeds the spread the truncated prior actually has about $d_p$, which is $0.8895$ at Week 2's values rather than $\Sigma_p = 1.0$. It shrinks when the squared distance is smaller. The drift vanishes where

$$\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[(\hat d - d_p)^2\big] \;=\; \Sigma_p\big(1 - d_p\zeta_p\big),$$

and an exercise shows that the right-hand side is the truncated prior's own second moment about $d_p$, $0.889504$ at Week 2's values.

The sensory variance grows when $\varepsilon_u^2 > \Pi_u$, that is when the squared residual $(u - g(d))^2$ exceeds $\Sigma_u$, and its drift vanishes where

$$\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[(u - g(\hat d))^2\big] \;=\; \Sigma_u .$$
(3.2.9)

At Week 2's observation the squared residual at the mode is $0.007020$, far below $\Sigma_u = 0.1$, so this single trial pushes $\Sigma_u$ down hard. Whether trials like it are typical is a question about the process, and Lesson 4 answers it.

The idea

Each stationary condition matches a moment of the model to the same moment of the inferred states: their mean to the prior's mean, their spread about $d_p$ to the prior's spread, and the spread of the residuals they leave to the sensory variance. The rules are only as good as the inferred states they average.

Those inferred states are modes, one point from each posterior. The averages that would make the conditions hold at the world's values are averages of whole posteriors: averaged over the process, the posterior means of $d$ reproduce the prior's mean exactly, and the modes in general do not. Squared deviations of modes also leave out each posterior's own spread. That omission alone would make them too small, but a mode is not its posterior's mean either, and on some trials the mode lies further from $d_p$ than the mean does, so the net effect has to be computed over the process. Lesson 4 derives both statements and computes what they cost.

4Locality

Week 2, Lesson 1 required a connection to change using only quantities available at its endpoints. In the circuit of Week 2, Lesson 4 the prior error unit compares the estimate with a node labelled expectation, which holds $d_p$. Bogacz (2017), in the section on learning model parameters, stores the same number differently: as the strength of a connection into the prior error unit from a unit whose activity is fixed at one. Bogacz defines the prior error as the estimate minus the prior location, the opposite sign to this course's $\varepsilon_p = (d_p - d)/\Sigma_p$, so his rule changes the connection by plus the product of the two activities. In this course's signs, (3.2.5) has two parts. The first, $-\varepsilon_p$, is minus the product of the activity at the receiving end, $\varepsilon_p$, and the activity at the sending end, which is one: the Hebbian form of Week 2, Lesson 1 with a constant presynaptic input, its sign set by how the error is coded. The second, $-\zeta_p$, is not a product of activities, and the next paragraph but one deals with it. So the full rule is a Hebbian term plus a correction that depends on the parameters.

The variances are the gains of the error units, which Week 2, Lesson 4 placed at the units. (3.2.7) uses the error unit's activity squared and the gain's own value. Both are available where the gain is stored.

The term $\zeta_p$ is a function of the two prior parameters alone. Computing it at the connection that stores $d_p$ needs $\Sigma_p$ there too, and in the circuit of Week 2, Lesson 4 $\Sigma_p$ is the gain of the error unit, stored somewhere else. So the rule is local only under an extra assumption: that the connection has its own copy of the prior variance, or that the two are stored together. With that assumption it meets the definition of local plasticity without being Hebbian. Bogacz's prior is not truncated and has no such term.

Several states at once

With several hidden states and observations the variances become covariance matrices, and the update for one entry involves the inverse of the whole matrix, which depends on every other entry. Bogacz notes that this breaks local plasticity and, in section 5, repairs it with an extra inhibitory unit per error unit. Week 5 takes up that construction.

5Variance, precision and log-variance

A parameter can be learned in any coordinate, and the choice changes the update.

DerivationThree coordinates for one parameter

Write $\delta_u = u - g(d)$ for the raw residual. (3.2.7) is $\partial\ell/\partial\Sigma_u = \tfrac12(\delta_u^2/\Sigma_u^2 - 1/\Sigma_u)$.

In the log-variance $\ln\Sigma_u$, the chain rule with $\mathrm d\Sigma_u/\mathrm d\ln\Sigma_u = \Sigma_u$ gives

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \ln\Sigma_u} \;=\; \Sigma_u\,\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \Sigma_u} \;=\; \tfrac12\left(\frac{\delta_u^2}{\Sigma_u} - 1\right) .$$
(3.2.10)

In the precision $\Pi_u = 1/\Sigma_u$, with $\mathrm d\Sigma_u/\mathrm d\Pi_u = -1/\Pi_u^2 = -\Sigma_u^2$,

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \Pi_u} \;=\; -\Sigma_u^2\,\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \Sigma_u} \;=\; \tfrac12\big(\Sigma_u - \delta_u^2\big) .$$

All three vanish at the same point, $\delta_u^2 = \Sigma_u$, because each is the first multiplied by a quantity that is never zero. That factor depends on the parameter but not on the trial, so the three drifts, averaged over the process at a fixed parameter value, also vanish at the same value.

What the three coordinates share is the zero of the drift. They do not share a trajectory, or the point a run with a finite rate rests at. A step of size $\beta$ in $\ln\Sigma_u$ multiplies $\Sigma_u$ by $e^{\beta S}$, where $S$ is (3.2.10); at the zero of the drift $S$ averages to zero, but $e^{\beta S}$ averages to more than one whenever $S$ varies, because the exponential is convex. The two runs' stationary means can differ at order $\beta$, the size of the mean shift Lesson 1 derives, while each run fluctuates about its own mean by order $\sqrt\beta$.

The update Bogacz proposes for learning the variance of a single error unit is a fourth variant. An inhibitory unit holds the raw residual $\delta_u$, the error unit holds $\varepsilon_u = \delta_u/\Sigma_u$, and the connection changes by $\beta(\varepsilon_u \delta_u - 1)$. Since $\varepsilon_u \delta_u - 1 = \delta_u^2/\Sigma_u - 1$, this is twice (3.2.10), applied to $\Sigma_u$ rather than to its logarithm: the plain gradient multiplied by $2\Sigma_u$. It has the same stationary point, and every quantity in it is the activity of one of the two units the connection joins.

CarefulA variance near zero

The plain update $\Sigma_u \leftarrow \Sigma_u + \beta\,\partial\ell/\partial\Sigma_u$ behaves badly when $\Sigma_u$ is small, because the gradient is not bounded there.

On a trial with zero residual the step is $-\beta/(2\Sigma_u)$, and it carries the variance below zero whenever $\beta/(2\Sigma_u) > \Sigma_u$, that is whenever $\Sigma_u < \sqrt{\beta/2}$. With $\beta = 0.002$ that is any variance below $0.0316$, and a negative variance has no meaning. On a trial with residual $\delta_u = 0.2$ and $\Sigma_u = 0.01$, the step is $\beta\cdot\tfrac12(0.2^2/0.01^2 - 1/0.01) = 0.3000$, many times the variance being updated. The same discrete step that is harmless at $\Sigma_u = 0.1$ is unstable near zero, which is the parameter version of the step-size bound of Week 2, Lesson 3.

Two repairs are standard. One is to take the step in the log-variance, $\Sigma_u \leftarrow \Sigma_u\exp\!\big(\beta\,\tfrac12(\delta_u^2/\Sigma_u - 1)\big)$, whose drift vanishes at the same value by (3.2.10) and which can never cross zero. It keeps the variance positive; it does not keep the steps small, since (3.2.10) still grows without bound as $\Sigma_u \to 0$ at a fixed nonzero residual. The simulations of this week use it for both variances. The other is to forbid small variances outright: Bogacz notes, in his section on learning model parameters, that Friston (2005) imposes a minimum value of one on the estimated variance, and in a footnote calls the constraint a hyperprior. Lesson 4 derives a smooth version.

Neither repair addresses why the variance is heading towards zero, which is sometimes the rule's fault rather than the step's. Lesson 4 shows when.

Exercise 3.2.1The second moment of the truncated prior

Show that $\int_0^\infty (x - d_p)^2 N(x)\,\mathrm dx / Z_p = \Sigma_p(1 - d_p\zeta_p)$, with $N$ and $\zeta_p$ as in this lesson, and conclude that the drift for $\Sigma_p$ vanishes where the average squared deviation of the inferred states from $d_p$ equals the prior's own.

Solution

Write $(x - d_p)^2 N = (x - d_p)\cdot(x - d_p)N = -\Sigma_p (x - d_p)\,\partial N/\partial x$ and integrate by parts:

$$-\Sigma_p\int_0^\infty (x - d_p)\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\,\mathrm dx \;=\; -\Sigma_p\Big[(x - d_p)N\Big]_0^\infty \;+\; \Sigma_p\int_0^\infty N\,\mathrm dx .$$

At infinity the bracket vanishes; at zero it is $(0 - d_p)N(0) = -d_pN(0)$, so the bracket contributes $-\Sigma_p\big(0 - (-d_pN(0))\big) = -\Sigma_p d_p N(0)$. The last integral is $\Sigma_p Z_p$. Dividing by $Z_p$,

$$\frac{1}{Z_p}\int_0^\infty (x - d_p)^2 N\,\mathrm dx \;=\; \Sigma_p - \Sigma_p d_p\zeta_p \;=\; \Sigma_p(1 - d_p\zeta_p),$$

which is $0.889504$ at Week 2's values.

For the second part, average (3.2.6) over trials and set it to zero: $\tfrac12\big(\mathbb E_{P^*}[\varepsilon_p^2] - \Pi_p\big) + d_p\zeta_p/(2\Sigma_p) = 0$. Multiply by $2\Sigma_p^2$ to get $\mathbb E_{P^*}[(\hat d - d_p)^2] - \Sigma_p + \Sigma_p d_p\zeta_p = 0$, which is the condition in the lesson, and its right-hand side is the moment just computed.

Exercise 3.2.2Precision as the learned quantity

A colleague learns $\Pi_u$ directly, with $\Pi_u \leftarrow \Pi_u + \beta\,\partial\ell/\partial\Pi_u$. Using the precision form derived above, say what happens on a trial with zero residual when $\Pi_u$ is very large, and on a trial with a large residual when $\Pi_u$ is very small. Compare with the variance update.

Solution

The step is $\beta\cdot\tfrac12(\Sigma_u - \delta_u^2) = \tfrac{\beta}{2}(1/\Pi_u - \delta_u^2)$.

With zero residual it is $\beta/(2\Pi_u)$: positive and small when $\Pi_u$ is large. Precision grows without bound but slowly.

With a residual $\delta_u$ held fixed, however large, and $\Pi_u \to 0$, the term $1/\Pi_u$ dominates and the step tends to $+\infty$: a small precision is thrown upwards, as a small variance was in the warning above. The approximation $-\beta\delta_u^2/2$ holds only when $\delta_u^2 \gg 1/\Pi_u$, which is the opposite regime. The next precision is $\Pi_u + \tfrac{\beta}{2}(1/\Pi_u - \delta_u^2)$. Multiplying it by the positive number $2/(\beta\Pi_u)$ shows that it is negative exactly when

$$\delta_u^2 \;>\; \frac{1}{\Pi_u} + \frac{2\Pi_u}{\beta} .$$

The right-hand side is large at both ends, so a negative precision needs a residual large compared with both the variance and $2\Pi_u/\beta$; a single outlier can supply one.

The two coordinates fail in mirror-image ways. In each, the gradient contains the reciprocal of the learned quantity and so is unbounded as that quantity goes to zero, and in each a large enough step crosses zero. The log-variance update is the same in either coordinate, since $\ln\Pi_u = -\ln\Sigma_u$. It cannot cross zero, because it multiplies by a positive factor, but its steps are still unbounded near zero at a fixed nonzero residual, so positivity is all it guarantees.
