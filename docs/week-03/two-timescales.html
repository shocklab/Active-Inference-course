Learning the model

Two timescales

Learning the parameters of the model by climbing the same log joint as inference, slowly and across many trials.

Week 3§145 min

Week 2 inferred a distance from one sound. Four numbers were fixed throughout: the prior location $d_p = 2.0$, the prior variance $\Sigma_p = 1.0$, the sensory variance $\Sigma_u = 0.1$ and, less visibly, the power of the source, which Week 2, Lesson 2 took as one "so that intensity is $1$ at unit distance". An animal is not given any of them. It hears many sounds over its life, and those sounds carry information about the four numbers as well as about each distance. This week the animal uses that information.

The method is the one Week 2 used for the distance: gradient ascent on the log joint. Only the argument changes.

1States and parameters

Definition 3.1.1Parameter

A parameter of a generative model is a quantity shared by every observation the model is used for. A hidden state belongs to one observation; a parameter belongs to all of them.

The distance $d$ is a state: each sound comes from a source at its own distance. The prior location, the two variances and the source power are parameters: the animal uses the same values for every sound it hears. The variances and the source power are positive; the prior location may be any real number.

Write $\theta$ for the source power, $\theta$ being the conventional letter for a parameter. The observation function of Week 2, Lesson 2 becomes

$$g(d, \theta) \;=\; \frac{\theta}{d^{2}} ,$$
(3.1.1)

and Week 2's model is the case $\theta = 1.0$. Week 1, Lesson 2 used $\theta$ locally for a sensor probability in the lamp example; from this week it is the source power. When a statement holds for any one of the four parameters $d_p, \Sigma_p, \Sigma_u, \theta$, we write $\omega$ for whichever is meant.

2The world and the model

Week 1, Lesson 2 separated the generative process, which produces the sounds, from the generative model, which the animal uses to interpret them. Learning only makes sense with both in view, because a learning rule is judged by where it takes the model's parameters given what the process does.

Definition 3.1.2Trial

A trial is one draw from the generative process followed by one round of inference. On trial $n$, for $n = 1, \dots, N$, the process places a source at distance $d_n$, drawn independently from a Gaussian with location $d_p^{*}$ and variance $\Sigma_p^{*}$ truncated to $d_n > 0$, and the animal hears

$$u_n \;=\; \frac{\theta^{*}}{d_n^{2}} \;+\; z_n ,$$

where $z_n \sim \mathcal N(0, \Sigma_u^{*})$. The noises $z_1, \dots, z_N$ are independent of one another and of every source distance, and the distances are independent of one another.

The star marks a quantity belonging to the process, following the convention of $P^{*}$ in Week 1, Lesson 2. The animal never sees a starred quantity. Its model has the same form with its own unstarred parameters, and when the model's parameters equal the starred ones the model is correct. Each experiment this week states its own process and the model the animal starts from, and the animal learns one parameter at a time while the other three stay where they started.

Two kinds of average appear from here on, and they are kept apart by their subscripts, as the notation page requires. $\mathbb E_{P^*}$ averages over trials of the process: over the source distance and the noise, and so over the observation. $\mathbb E_{d\mid u}$ averages over the model's posterior on the distance, for one observation $u$ and the parameters the animal holds at the time.

3The log joint with its constants restored

Week 2, Lesson 3 wrote the log joint for one trial as

$$\ell \;=\; -\frac{(d - d_p)^2}{2\Sigma_p} \;-\; \frac{(u - g(d))^2}{2\Sigma_u} \;+\; \text{const}$$

and justified dropping the constant because it contained no $d$. The constant does contain the parameters. Restoring it from Week 2, Lesson 3's expansion of the truncated prior and the Gaussian likelihood:

$$\ell(d, u; \omega) \;=\; -\frac{(d - d_p)^2}{2\Sigma_p} \;-\; \tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_p) \;-\; \ln Z_p \;-\; \frac{\big(u - \theta/d^{2}\big)^2}{2\Sigma_u} \;-\; \tfrac12\ln(2\pi\Sigma_u) ,$$
(3.1.2)

for $d > 0$, with $Z_p = \int_0^\infty \mathcal N(x; d_p, \Sigma_p)\,\mathrm dx$ as in Week 2, Lesson 2. $Z_p$ depends on $d_p$ and $\Sigma_p$. At Week 2's values it is $0.977250$, and at Week 2's observation $u = 0.5$ and its mode $\hat d = 1.5500$ the full log joint is $-0.7999$.

Over $N$ trials the joint density of all the states and observations is a product, because the trials are independent given the parameters, so its logarithm is a sum:

$$\mathcal L \;=\; \sum_{n=1}^{N} \ell(d_n, u_n; \omega) .$$
(3.1.3)

Here $\mathcal L$ is a function of the $N$ distances and the four parameters. The script letter keeps it apart from the expected losses of Week 2, Lesson 5.

4One objective, two kinds of argument

DerivationThe gradient of the total log joint

Differentiate (3.1.3) with respect to the distance of one trial, $d_m$. Only the term $n = m$ contains $d_m$, so every other term differentiates to zero:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial d_m} \;=\; \frac{\partial \ell(d_m, u_m; \omega)}{\partial d_m} \;=\; \varepsilon_{p,m} \;+\; g'(d_m)\,\varepsilon_{u,m} ,$$

which is Week 2's gradient for trial $m$ alone, with the prediction errors of Week 2, Lesson 3 evaluated on that trial. Inference on one trial needs nothing from any other trial.

Differentiate instead with respect to a parameter $\omega$. Every term contains $\omega$, so none drops:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \omega} \;=\; \sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{\partial \ell(d_n, u_n; \omega)}{\partial \omega} .$$
(3.1.4)

The gradient for a parameter is a sum over every trial the parameter is used in.

The two kinds of argument are treated differently because of this difference, not because they are different kinds of object. A state's gradient is available within its trial. A parameter's gradient is spread across trials, and an animal that hears one sound at a time never holds the whole sum.

5Settling first

Suppose inference on trial $n$ is run until it stops, at the mode $\hat d_n$. The log joint at that point is a function of the observation and the parameters alone.

Definition 3.1.3Settled log joint

The settled log joint of one trial is

$$\hat\ell(u; \omega) \;=\; \ell\big(\hat d(u; \omega), u; \omega\big) ,$$

where $\hat d(u; \omega)$ is the mode of Week 2, Lesson 3 for observation $u$ and parameters $\omega$.

The mode moves when a parameter moves, so $\hat\ell$ depends on $\omega$ in two ways: directly, and through $\hat d$. Its derivative nevertheless needs only the direct one.

DerivationThe envelope theorem

Assume the mode is a strict local maximum, $\ell'' < 0$ there, so that by the implicit function theorem $\hat d$ is a differentiable function of $\omega$ near the current value. By the chain rule,

$$\frac{\mathrm d\hat\ell}{\mathrm d\omega} \;=\; \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \omega}\bigg|_{d = \hat d} \;+\; \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial d}\bigg|_{d = \hat d}\,\frac{\mathrm d\hat d}{\mathrm d\omega} .$$

The first term is the change in $\ell$ from moving $\omega$ with the state held fixed. The second is the change from the state moving in response. The mode is a stationary point, so $\partial\ell/\partial d = 0$ at $d = \hat d$, and the second term vanishes whatever $\mathrm d\hat d/\mathrm d\omega$ is:

$$\frac{\mathrm d\hat\ell}{\mathrm d\omega} \;=\; \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \omega}\bigg|_{d = \hat d} .$$
(3.1.5)

So the slope of the settled log joint can be read off without knowing how the mode would move. The animal evaluates the partial derivative at the state it has already inferred.

The mode does move, and the same implicit-function argument gives how far.

DerivationHow the mode moves with the prior location

Write $\ell_d$ for $\partial\ell/\partial d$. The mode satisfies $\ell_d\big(\hat d(\omega), \omega\big) = 0$ for every $\omega$ near the current value. Differentiating this identity in $\omega$ by the chain rule,

$$\ell''\,\frac{\mathrm d\hat d}{\mathrm d\omega} \;+\; \frac{\partial \ell_d}{\partial\omega} \;=\; 0 , \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{\mathrm d\hat d}{\mathrm d\omega} \;=\; \frac{\partial \ell_d/\partial\omega}{-\ell''} ,$$

with both derivatives evaluated at $d = \hat d$. For the prior location, the only term of $\ell_d$ that contains $d_p$ is $(d_p - d)/\Sigma_p$; the normaliser $\ln Z_p$ depends on $d_p$ but not on $d$, so it drops out of $\ell_d$. Hence $\partial\ell_d/\partial d_p = 1/\Sigma_p$ and

$$\frac{\partial\hat d}{\partial d_p} \;=\; \frac{1/\Sigma_p}{-\ell''} \;>\; 0 ,$$
(3.1.6)

positive because $-\ell'' > 0$ at a strict maximum.

Moving the prior location outward moves the mode outward. At Week 2's mode $\ell'' = -3.0132$, so (3.1.6) is $0.3319$, which agrees with a finite-difference derivative of the mode to six decimal places.

Two cautions about "the mode". Week 2's posterior can have more than one local maximum, and the envelope theorem holds at whichever one inference stopped on; $\hat\ell$ is the log joint on that branch. And a climb with finite steps can reach a different local maximum than the continuous ascent would, as Week 2, Lesson 3 showed. The simulations this week run Week 2's capped ascent until a step falls below $10^{-12}$, giving up after 2000 steps. The climb starts at the prior location when that is positive, and otherwise at the mean of the truncated prior, which is positive for every real $d_p$. In the 9 runs whose numbers the lessons quote, every trial met that stopping rule, and every settled state agreed with the highest mode of its trial, found by a separate grid search, to within $1.9 \times 10^{-11}$. That is a check of those runs, not a guarantee for every slider setting; the widgets add a readout row if a trial runs out of steps. A trial that runs out of steps changes no parameter whose update is read at the mode, in the widgets, the notebooks and values.py alike, so that an unfinished state never feeds a learning rule. The posterior-averaged rule of Lesson 4 does not use the mode, and it updates on every trial.

6The log joint in place of the evidence

The number that says how well a set of parameters accounts for a sound is the evidence, $p(u; \omega) = \int_0^\infty p(d, u; \omega)\,\mathrm dd$, the denominator of Bayes' rule that Week 1, Lesson 5 examined. A statistician fitting the four parameters to recorded sounds would maximise the sum of $\ln p(u_n; \omega)$ over trials. Its derivative needs that integral on every trial, or, as Lesson 4 shows, an average over the whole posterior, which is the same integral in another form. Week 2 avoided the integral for the state by climbing the log joint instead, and the animal avoids it for the parameters the same way: $\hat\ell$ needs the two factors of the joint, evaluated at one point it has already found. The price is that $\hat\ell$ is not $\ln p(u; \omega)$, so climbing one need not climb the other. Lesson 4 computes the difference, and Week 4 replaces $\hat\ell$ by an objective that includes the missing term.

This is a statement about exact stationary points, so check it. At Week 2's observation, a finite-difference derivative of $\hat\ell$ with respect to $\Sigma_u$, with the mode recomputed at each perturbed value, gives $-4.648993$. The partial derivative at the mode, which Lesson 2 derives, gives $-4.648993$.

Now stop inference early. After $3$ of Week 2's steps from the prior location the estimate is at $d = 1.9096$, where $\partial\ell/\partial d = -0.5580$, not zero. The total derivative of $\ell$ at that point, with the three steps rerun at each perturbed $\Sigma_u$, is $-2.9627$, while the partial derivative is $-2.4516$. The second term of the chain rule has not vanished. The partial derivative is still the slope of $\ell$ with the state held where the three steps left it, which is a direction of coordinate ascent, but it is not the slope of the objective the three steps define, $\ell$ evaluated wherever three steps from the prior location land.

7The slow update

Definition 3.1.4Learning rate

On each trial the animal first settles its state, then changes each parameter by a small step along the partial derivative at the settled state:

$$\omega \;\leftarrow\; \omega \;+\; \beta\,\frac{\partial \ell(d, u_n; \omega)}{\partial \omega}\bigg|_{d = \hat d_n} .$$
(3.1.7)

The learning rate $\beta > 0$ plays the role for parameters that $\eta$ plays for the state. Each parameter has its own, with units that make the step carry the parameter's units. Week 2, Lesson 1 used $\beta$ for the step size of a weight update, and the meaning is the same.

By (3.1.5), each step of (3.1.7) is a step up the settled log joint of the current trial. By (3.1.4), the direction that would increase the total is the sum of these per-trial directions. Taking one small step per trial, rather than summing first, replaces the sum by a running accumulation: after many trials with a small $\beta$, the parameter has moved roughly as it would under ascent on the average of $\hat\ell$ over the trials it has seen, with fluctuations from the trials it happened to see last. The next section makes this exact in a case that can be solved.

8A running average

Take Week 2, Lesson 4's linear comparison: $g(d) = d$, a Gaussian prior on the whole line, and the prior location as the only parameter being learned. Week 2, Lesson 4 derived the mode,

$$\hat d_n \;=\; \frac{\Pi_p d_p + \Pi_u u_n}{\Pi_p + \Pi_u} .$$
DerivationThe slow update for the prior location is an average of observations

On the whole line there is no truncation, so $Z_p = 1$ for every $d_p$ and (3.1.2) gives $\partial\ell/\partial d_p = (d - d_p)/\Sigma_p$. At the mode,

$$\hat d_n - d_p \;=\; \frac{\Pi_p d_p + \Pi_u u_n - (\Pi_p + \Pi_u)\,d_p}{\Pi_p + \Pi_u} \;=\; \frac{\Pi_u}{\Pi_p + \Pi_u}\,(u_n - d_p) .$$

Multiply numerator and denominator by $\Sigma_p\Sigma_u$, using $\Pi_u\Sigma_p\Sigma_u = \Sigma_p$ and $(\Pi_p + \Pi_u)\Sigma_p\Sigma_u = \Sigma_u + \Sigma_p$:

$$\hat d_n - d_p \;=\; \frac{\Sigma_p}{\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u}\,(u_n - d_p) .$$

Substituting into (3.1.7),

$$d_p \;\leftarrow\; d_p + \frac{\beta}{\Sigma_p}\cdot\frac{\Sigma_p}{\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u}\,(u_n - d_p) \;=\; (1 - \kappa)\,d_p + \kappa\,u_n, \qquad \kappa \;=\; \frac{\beta}{\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u} .$$
(3.1.8)

Applying (3.1.8) once per trial unwinds it. Write $d_p^{(n)}$ for the location after trial $n$, with $d_p^{(0)}$ the starting value. The first two trials give

$$d_p^{(1)} \;=\; (1-\kappa)\,d_p^{(0)} + \kappa u_1 , \qquad d_p^{(2)} \;=\; (1-\kappa)^{2}\,d_p^{(0)} + \kappa(1-\kappa)\,u_1 + \kappa u_2 ,$$

and substituting the expression for $d_p^{(n-1)}$ into (3.1.8) each time gives, by induction,

$$d_p^{(n)} \;=\; (1-\kappa)^{n}\,d_p^{(0)} \;+\; \kappa\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(1-\kappa)^{k}\,u_{n-k} .$$
(3.1.9)

For $\kappa \neq 0$ the geometric series gives $\kappa\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(1-\kappa)^{k} = 1 - (1-\kappa)^{n}$, so the weights on the observations and the weight $(1-\kappa)^{n}$ on the starting value add to one.

The factor $1/\Sigma_p$ in the update cancelled against the $\Sigma_p$ in the shrinkage factor, though $\Sigma_p$ is still in $\kappa$. For $0 < \kappa < 1$ the prior location is an exponentially weighted average of the raw observations: by (3.1.9), trial $n - k$ carries weight $\kappa(1-\kappa)^{k}$, so older trials fade geometrically, and the starting value's weight $(1-\kappa)^{n}$ goes to zero. (At $\kappa = 1$ the memory is the last trial alone, and for $\kappa > 1$ the weights alternate in sign.) The inferred states dropped out of the final expression, but only because the mode is linear in $u_n$ here; in the nonlinear model they do not.

The average of (3.1.8) over the process is $\mathbb E_{P^*}[d_p'] = (1-\kappa)\,d_p + \kappa\,\mathbb E_{P^*}[u]$, and in this linear world $\mathbb E_{P^*}[u]$ is the world's location $d_p^{*}$, because the noise has mean zero. The expected step vanishes at $d_p = d_p^{*}$ and nowhere else: in the linear world the location is learned without bias. An exercise asks for the proof in full, and Lesson 4 shows why the nonlinear world is different.

The letter $\kappa$ is chosen deliberately. Week 1, Lesson 1's regulator removed a fraction $\kappa$ of the displacement on each step, and (3.1.8) removes a fraction $\kappa$ of the gap between $d_p$ and the newest observation. The same algebra gives the same answers.

DerivationThe spread of a learned parameter

Suppose the observations have mean $\bar u$ and variance $\sigma_u^2$, write $x_n = d_p - \bar u$ for the deviation after trial $n$, and subtract $\bar u$ from both sides of (3.1.8):

$$x_{n+1} \;=\; (1 - \kappa)\,x_n + \kappa\,(u_n - \bar u) .$$

The newest observation is independent of $x_n$, which was built from earlier ones. Taking variances, the cross term vanishes:

$$\operatorname{Var}(x_{n+1}) \;=\; (1-\kappa)^2\operatorname{Var}(x_n) + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2 .$$

For $0 < \kappa < 2$ the factor $(1-\kappa)^2$ is below one, the variances settle, and at stationarity the two are equal; call them $V_x$. Then $V_x\big[1 - (1-\kappa)^2\big] = \kappa^2\sigma_u^2$, and $1 - (1-\kappa)^2 = \kappa(2 - \kappa)$, so

$$V_x \;=\; \frac{\kappa}{2 - \kappa}\,\sigma_u^2 .$$
(3.1.10)

Here $\sigma_u^2$ is the variance of the observations as the world produces them, the sum of the spread of sources and the sensory noise. Week 1, Lesson 1 found the same expression for its regulated walk, with $\kappa^2\sigma_u^2$ in the role of the noise.

The learning rate therefore trades speed against steadiness. The half-life of an old trial's influence is $\ln 2/[-\ln(1-\kappa)]$ trials, and the standard deviation of $d_p$ about its target is $\sqrt{\kappa/(2-\kappa)}$ times that of the observations. With Week 2's variances in this linear world and $\beta = 0.02$: $\kappa = 0.01818$, a half-life of 37.8 trials, and a standard deviation of $0.1005$ for $d_p$. A simulation of this linear world with the same rate gives $0.1005$. For small $\kappa$, halving $\beta$ about doubles the memory and reduces the fluctuation by a factor of about $\sqrt 2$.

9Resting points of a learning rule

Every learning rule this week has the form (3.1.7): a step $\beta F(\omega, u_n)$, where $F$ is computed from the trial's observation and the current parameter. The running average is the one case solved exactly. The general case is judged by one function.

Definition 3.1.5Drift

The drift of a learning rule is its step per unit rate averaged over the process at a fixed parameter value,

$$\varphi(\omega) \;=\; \mathbb E_{P^*}\big[F(\omega, u)\big] .$$

For the rule (3.1.7), $F(\omega, u) = \partial\ell/\partial\omega$ at $d = \hat d(u; \omega)$.

DerivationA learning rule fluctuates about a zero of its drift

Write the step as its average plus a remainder, $F(\omega_n, u_n) = \varphi(\omega_n) + \xi_n$, where $\xi_n$ has mean zero over the process given $\omega_n$, because $u_n$ is a fresh draw independent of everything that set $\omega_n$. Suppose $\varphi(\omega^{\circ}) = 0$, and expand $\varphi$ about $\omega^{\circ}$ to first order: $\varphi(\omega) \approx \varphi'(\omega^{\circ})(\omega - \omega^{\circ})$. This is an approximation, valid only while $\omega_n$ stays in a neighbourhood of $\omega^{\circ}$ where the first-order term dominates, and it treats the variance of $\xi_n$ as fixed, though in general it depends on $\omega_n$. With $x_n = \omega_n - \omega^{\circ}$,

$$x_{n+1} \;=\; x_n + \beta\varphi'(\omega^{\circ})\,x_n + \beta\xi_n \;=\; (1 - \kappa)\,x_n + \beta\xi_n, \qquad \kappa \;=\; -\beta\,\varphi'(\omega^{\circ}) .$$

This is the recursion of (3.1.8), with $\beta\xi_n$ in place of $\kappa(u_n - \bar u)$. The derivation of (3.1.10) used the independence of the newest term from $x_n$ only to drop the cross term, and a mean of zero given the history is enough for that. Write $\mathcal H_n$ for everything that happened before trial $n$; it fixes $x_n$ and every earlier remainder $\xi_m$, $m < n$. Averaging first over $u_n$ with $\mathcal H_n$ held fixed, then over $\mathcal H_n$ (the tower property of conditional expectation),

$$\mathbb E[x_n\xi_n] \;=\; \mathbb E\big[x_n\,\mathbb E(\xi_n \mid \mathcal H_n)\big] \;=\; 0, \qquad \mathbb E[\xi_m\xi_n] \;=\; \mathbb E\big[\xi_m\,\mathbb E(\xi_n \mid \mathcal H_n)\big] \;=\; 0 \quad (m < n) .$$

So the cross term still vanishes, and remainders on different trials are uncorrelated. Within that approximation, three conclusions follow from the running average. If $\varphi'(\omega^{\circ}) < 0$ and $0 < \kappa < 2$, the expected deviation $\mathbb E_{P^*}[x_n]$ shrinks by a factor $1 - \kappa$ per trial, so $\omega^{\circ}$ attracts. If $\varphi'(\omega^{\circ}) > 0$, then $\kappa < 0$, the factor exceeds one and $\omega^{\circ}$ repels. And at an attracting zero the deviation has stationary variance $\beta^2\sigma_\xi^2/[\kappa(2-\kappa)]$ by (3.1.10), which is about $\beta\sigma_\xi^2/(2|\varphi'(\omega^{\circ})|)$ for small $\beta$, with $\sigma_\xi^2$ the variance of $\xi$ near $\omega^{\circ}$.

The stationary variance is of order $\beta$, so the standard deviation of one value $\omega_n$ about $\omega^{\circ}$ is of order $\sqrt\beta$. The expansion also dropped the term $\tfrac12\varphi''(\omega^{\circ})x_n^2$, which moves the stationary mean. Near an attracting zero, and assuming $x_n$ has a stationary distribution with finite moments, average $x_{n+1} = x_n + \beta\big[\varphi'x_n + \tfrac12\varphi''x_n^2 + \xi_n\big]$ over it. The left side and the first term on the right have the same average, and $\xi_n$ averages to zero, so to leading order

$$0 \;\approx\; \varphi'(\omega^{\circ})\,\mathbb E[x] + \tfrac12\varphi''(\omega^{\circ})\,\mathbb E[x^2], \qquad \mathbb E[x] \;\approx\; -\frac{\varphi''(\omega^{\circ})}{2\varphi'(\omega^{\circ})}\operatorname{Var}(x) ,$$

where replacing $\mathbb E[x^2]$ by $\operatorname{Var}(x)$ neglects $\mathbb E[x]^2$, which is smaller still. The stationary mean is therefore displaced from the zero by an amount of order $\beta$.

Three quantities have now appeared, and they scale differently with the rate. One value of a parameter fluctuates about the zero of the drift with a standard deviation of order $\sqrt\beta$. The mean of those values, over the stationary distribution, can sit off the zero by order $\beta$ when the drift is curved. And the average of one finite run differs from that stationary mean by a sampling error, which depends on how long the run is and on how strongly successive values are correlated. The running average has no mean shift: its drift is exactly linear, so its stationary mean is exactly the zero. It shows the first quantity clearly, since its standard deviation in the linear world is $0.1005$ at $\beta = 0.02$, $0.0501$ at $\beta = 0.005$ and $0.0250$ at $\beta = 0.00125$: dividing the rate by four halves the fluctuation.

So a rule is judged by where its drift vanishes and by the sign of the drift's slope there. The running average is the case $F = (u - d_p)/(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$, with $\varphi'= -1/(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$ and $\kappa = \beta/(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$, as before. When the lessons that follow say a rule is biased, they mean its drift vanishes somewhere other than the world's value. A run with a fixed rate fluctuates about that point, and the average of one run is evidence about the zero of the drift, not a measurement of it.

10Timescale separation

The scheme needs two things. Inference must settle within each trial, so that (3.1.5) applies. And a parameter must change little over the many trials its average is taken across, so that a single loud or quiet sound does not set it. The first is a condition on the number of inference steps per trial; the second is $\kappa \ll 1$.

CarefulWhat the separation of timescales is doing

For a fixed set of recorded observations, settling every state against the current parameters and then stepping the parameters is alternating ascent on (3.1.3): the states are profiled out, and

$$\max_{d_1, \dots, d_N}\mathcal L \;=\; \sum_{n=1}^{N}\hat\ell(u_n; \omega)$$

when each settled state is the highest mode of its trial. The two speeds do not change the criterion. They change the path taken through it, and so which local maximum is reached, as finite steps did for the state in Week 2, Lesson 3.

That criterion has no global maximum. Put every $d_n$ at $d_p$. The prior terms become $-\tfrac N2\ln(2\pi\Sigma_p) - N\ln Z_p$, and the sensory terms do not involve $\Sigma_p$. As $\Sigma_p \to 0$ with $d_p > 0$ fixed, $Z_p \to 1$ and $-\tfrac N2 \ln \Sigma_p \to +\infty$, so $\mathcal L$ grows without bound along a path that declares every source to be exactly where it was expected and the prior to be perfectly sharp. When every recorded observation is positive, the same happens with $\Sigma_u \to 0$ along states that reproduce each one exactly, $\theta/d_n^{2} = u_n$. A single negative observation cannot be reproduced by any distance, leaves a nonzero residual, and sends that path to $-\infty$ instead; Gaussian noise does produce negative observations.

An objective unbounded above can still have local maxima, and the slow dynamics look for one. What they reach is at best a local maximum of the average of $\hat\ell$, never a global one. Lesson 4 finds where the drift of each parameter vanishes and what the unboundedness does there.

Three timescales

Buckley et al. (2017), section 8, separate three: states fastest, then the parameters of the observation and dynamics functions, then the precisions slowest. Their parameter dynamics are written in second-order form, with the accumulated gradient setting the rate of change; an exercise examines what that does.

11The two timescales running at once

In the widget, the process places sources at location $d_p^{*} = 3.0$, further away than the animal expects. Everything else in the process is Week 2's model, except that the ear is sharper: $\Sigma_u^{*} = \Sigma_u = 0.01$ for both process and model, Week 2's sharp setting. The animal learns $d_p$ alone, with $\beta = 0.01$, over 2000 trials.

On the first trial the state settles in 178 steps, at $d = 2.8819$. Across trials the prior location climbs: it is at $2.3903$ after 200 trials, and over the second half of the run it averages $2.4946$ with a standard deviation of $0.0403$.

It does not reach $3.0$. The drift vanishes at $d_p = 2.5019$, $-16.6$ per cent from the world's location, and that is where the run fluctuates. Set the ear back to Week 2's $\Sigma_u = 0.1$ and the drift vanishes at $1.9274$, $-35.8$ per cent from it: the prior location moves down, below where it started, while the sources have moved further away.

The sharp ear is not sharp about distance at this range. What the ear says about $d$ is set by the slope of the link, and its precision about $d$ near a source at distance $d$ is $g'(d)^2/\Sigma_u = 4\theta^2/(d^{6}\Sigma_u)$, which falls as $d^{-6}$. At $d = 3.0$ with $\Sigma_u = 0.01$ it is $0.5487$, less than the prior's precision of $1.0$. At Week 2's location and ear it is $0.6250$. So the posterior for a distant source is wide whichever ear the animal has, and wide posteriors are where the mode and the posterior mean part company. Nothing in this lesson says what that does to learning. Lesson 4 does.

Exercise 3.1.1One trial or many

Show from (3.1.4) that if the animal could store all $N$ settled states and take a full gradient step on the sum, a learning rate $\beta/N$ would give the same expected step as $N$ single-trial steps of size $\beta/N$ taken with the parameter held fixed. Then say what changes when the parameter is updated after each trial instead of being held fixed.

Solution

With the parameter held at $\omega$ across the $N$ trials, the $N$ single-trial steps add up to $(\beta/N)\sum_n \partial\ell_n/\partial\omega$, which is a full step of size $\beta/N$ on (3.1.4). They are the same sum taken in a different order.

When the parameter is updated after each trial, trial $n$'s derivative is evaluated at the value left by trials $1$ to $n-1$, and its settled state was inferred with that value too. The order of the trials now matters, and the result is a different iteration. For small $\beta$ the parameter changes little over any stretch of trials, so each derivative is evaluated near a common value and the two schemes nearly agree; (3.1.8) is the exactly solvable case, in which later observations carry more weight than earlier ones.
