Learning the model

Learning the link

The source power sits inside the prediction, so its update is the sensory error times the input that carried it. What that update drives to zero, and what three parameters learned together cannot tell apart.

Week 3§345 min

The prediction of Lesson 1 is $g(d, \theta) = \theta/d^{2}$: a source of power $\theta$ at distance $d$. Week 2 fixed $\theta = 1.0$. This lesson lets the animal learn it, and in doing so meets the plasticity rule that gives predictive coding its claim to biological plausibility, along with two things that rule does not do.

1The derivative in the link

Write the link as the power multiplied by a shape,

$$g(d, \theta) \;=\; \theta\,h(d), \qquad h(d) \;=\; \frac{1}{d^{2}} ,$$
(3.3.1)

where the link shape $h$ is the part of the prediction that depends on the state. The letter is chosen because $h$ is what $g$ has left once its parameter is taken out.

DerivationThe update for the source power

$\theta$ appears in the log joint of Lesson 1 only through the sensory term $-\big(u - \theta h(d)\big)^2/(2\Sigma_u)$. By the chain rule, with $\partial g/\partial\theta = h(d)$,

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} \;=\; \frac{u - \theta h(d)}{\Sigma_u}\cdot h(d) \;=\; \varepsilon_u\,h(d) .$$
(3.3.2)

The same steps hold for any link that is linear in its parameter: if $g(d, \theta) = \theta h(d)$ for some shape $h$, the derivative is $\varepsilon_u h(d)$ whatever $h$ is.

At Week 2's observation and mode, $h(\hat d) = 0.4162$ and $\varepsilon_u = 0.8379$, so (3.3.2) is $0.3487$, positive: the sound was louder than the settled estimate predicts, and raising the source power would explain more of it.

For a link that is not linear in its parameter the chain rule gives $\varepsilon_u\,\partial g/\partial\theta$, and $\partial g/\partial\theta$ may depend on $\theta$ as well as $d$. The form $\varepsilon_u h(d)$ is a property of the linear case, which is also the case Bogacz (2017) treats in its section on learning the relationship between variables.

2A Hebbian rule

Give the circuit of Week 2, Lesson 4 one more unit, whose activity is $h(d)$. It reads the estimate and applies the shape, so its activity is fixed by $d$ alone. Its output reaches the sensory error unit through a connection of strength $\theta$, so the sensory error unit receives the prediction $\theta h(d)$ and subtracts it from $u$.

Week 2, Lesson 1 defined local plasticity as a change computed from the activities at the two ends of a connection and its own strength, and the Hebbian rule as the product of those two activities. The connection of strength $\theta$ runs from the link-shape unit, with activity $h(d)$, to the sensory error unit, with activity $\varepsilon_u$. (3.3.2) is exactly their product. Bogacz makes the same observation. The update needs nothing from the rest of the network, and in particular it does not need to know what the shape $h$ is: the unit at the sending end has already computed it.

The two parameters that enter a prediction follow one pattern. Each error is a difference divided by a variance, and the update for a parameter in that difference is minus the error times the derivative of the difference with respect to the parameter. For $\theta$ the difference is $u - \theta h(d)$, whose derivative is $-h(d)$, and the update is $+\varepsilon_u h(d)$. For the prior location the difference is $d_p - d$, whose derivative is one, and the update is $-\varepsilon_p$: the product of the error and a sending activity fixed at one, with a minus sign. The prior location also carries the truncation term $-\zeta_p$ of Lesson 2, which is not a product of activities. This is the payoff of the error-unit form of Week 2, Lesson 4: apart from that correction, the quantities the gradient of the log joint needs are activities that the inference circuit already has.

CarefulOne parameter, two connections

$\theta$ appears twice in the circuit. It scales the prediction on the way to the error unit, and it scales the error on the way back, since the return path carries $g'(d) = \theta h'(d)$. A circuit built from connections has two separate strengths there, call them $\theta_{\downarrow}$ and $\theta_{\uparrow}$, and the derivation of Week 2, Lesson 3 assumed they are equal.

Suppose each is updated by (3.3.2). Bogacz notes that the return connection can compute it locally, since the estimate unit at its receiving end fixes $h(d)$. Both then receive the same increment $\beta\,\varepsilon_u h(\hat d)$ on every trial, so

$$\theta_{\downarrow}^{(n+1)} - \theta_{\uparrow}^{(n+1)} \;=\; \big(\theta_{\downarrow}^{(n)} + \beta\varepsilon_u h\big) - \big(\theta_{\uparrow}^{(n)} + \beta\varepsilon_u h\big) \;=\; \theta_{\downarrow}^{(n)} - \theta_{\uparrow}^{(n)} .$$

Learning preserves the difference exactly. If the two strengths start equal they stay equal, and if they start unequal they never become equal.

What the inner loop does with unequal strengths can be computed. It climbs

$$\frac{d_p - d}{\Sigma_p} \;+\; \frac{u - \theta_{\downarrow}h(d)}{\Sigma_u}\,\theta_{\uparrow}h'(d) ,$$

the prediction made with the forward strength and the error returned with the other. For positive strengths, write $\Sigma_u\theta_{\uparrow} = \theta_{\downarrow}\cdot\Sigma_u\theta_{\uparrow}/\theta_{\downarrow}$ and set $\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}} = \Sigma_u\theta_{\downarrow}/\theta_{\uparrow}$. The second term becomes $\big(u - \theta_{\downarrow}h(d)\big)\,\theta_{\downarrow}h'(d)/\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}$, which is the derivative in $d$ of $-\big(u - \theta_{\downarrow}h(d)\big)^2/(2\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}})$. So the loop climbs the log joint of a model with power $\theta_{\downarrow}$ and sensory variance $\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}$ in place of $\Sigma_u$, and settles at that model's mode, which need not equal the mode of the model the animal is meant to hold. (The two coincide when $d_p > 0$ on a trial with $u = \theta_{\downarrow}/d_p^2$, where both errors vanish at $d = d_p$ whatever the sensory variance.) A mismatch between the two strengths acts as a mis-set sensory variance, and because learning adds the same amount to both, the ratio $\theta_{\downarrow}/\theta_{\uparrow}$, and with it $\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}$, changes as the power is learned. With a single state every such field is the derivative of something; with several states a mismatch between forward and return weights need not give the gradient of any function, a case Week 5 meets. The rule is local, but it relies on a symmetry that nothing local enforces.

3What learning drives to zero

Setting the drift of (3.3.2) to zero, the stationary condition is

$$\mathbb E_{P^*}\big[\varepsilon_u\,h(\hat d)\big] \;=\; 0 .$$
(3.3.3)

This is a condition on the learning signal $\varepsilon_u h(\hat d)$, which weights each trial's sensory error by the link shape at its estimate. It is not a condition on the sensory error alone, nor on the prior error. Trials with near sources have large $h(\hat d)$ and count for more.

The widget below sets up a world whose sources are twice as powerful as the animal believes, $\theta^{*} = 2$ against the animal's starting $\theta = 1$, with every other parameter as in Week 2. It shows the learned power across trials and the running averages of the two errors and of the learning signal over the last $200$ trials.

With learning off, over the last $200$ of $2000$ trials the average sensory error is $-0.035$, close to zero, and the average prior error is $0.646$. Sounds come in louder than the animal's model predicts for sources at their usual distances, and the inner loop explains them by placing the sources nearer than the prior expects. The mismatch shows up in the prior error, and the sensory error, which looks at the link, averages to almost nothing. A small average sensory error is not evidence that the link is right.

The learning signal is not fooled: its average is $0.2271$, because the trials inferred as near carry large $h(\hat d)$ and, on those, the sensory error is positive. With learning on, the power rises to $1.846$ by the last trial, averaging $1.730$ over the second half, and the window averages of the learning signal fall from $0.1327$ over the first $200$ trials to $0.0310$ over the last.

The errors do not fall to zero with it. Over the last $200$ trials the average prior error is $0.351$ and the average sensory error $-0.448$. With the true power given to the animal from the start and no learning at all, the averages over every trial are $0.218$ and $-0.681$. Neither error averages to zero even when the model is exactly right, and two separate effects are in those numbers.

DerivationThe errors at the true parameters, averaged over the posterior

Suppose the model equals the process and each error is averaged over the posterior instead of read at the mode. Averaging the posterior over the observations the process produces gives back the joint distribution of distance and observation, which is the process itself, so the process average of a posterior average is the process average of the quantity averaged. For the sensory error that is the average of $(u - g(d))/\Sigma_u$ over the process, the average noise divided by $\Sigma_u$, which is zero. For the prior error it is $(d_p - \mathbb E_{P^*}[d])/\Sigma_p$, and the mean of the truncated prior is $d_p + \Sigma_p\zeta_p$ by Lesson 2, so the average is $-\zeta_p = -0.0552$.

So averaged over posteriors, the sensory error at the truth is zero and the prior error is slightly negative, because the truncated prior's mean lies above its location. The simulated averages, $0.218$ and $-0.681$, are far from both. The difference is the mode: the errors are measured from one point of each posterior, and the mode of a skewed posterior is not its mean. What does average to zero at a stable resting point of learning is the drift, here the weighted signal of (3.3.3). A zero average of a derivative is not a minimum of the errors, and "learning minimises prediction error" is not what this rule does.

The power learned is below the truth: the drift vanishes at $1.7739$, $-11.3$ per cent from $\theta^{*}$. Lesson 4 traces the gap to the difference between the mode and the posterior average, and finds that its sign for $\theta$ depends on the world: in a world equal to Week 2's model the same rule overshoots the power, by $12.0$ per cent.

4Three parameters that cannot be told apart

The widget learns $\theta$ alone. Learning $\theta$, $d_p$ and $\Sigma_p$ together runs into a problem that no learning rule can solve, because it is a property of the model.

DerivationA rescaling the evidence cannot see

Take any $c > 0$ and define $d' = c\,d$. If $d$ has the truncated prior with location $d_p$ and variance $\Sigma_p$, then $d'$ has a truncated Gaussian prior with location $c\,d_p$ and variance $c^{2}\Sigma_p$: a positive multiple of a Gaussian is Gaussian with the mean and standard deviation multiplied by $c$, and $d > 0$ exactly when $d' > 0$. The prediction in terms of $d'$ is

$$\frac{\theta}{d^{2}} \;=\; \frac{\theta}{(d'/c)^{2}} \;=\; \frac{c^{2}\theta}{d'^{2}} .$$

So the model with parameters $(\theta, d_p, \Sigma_p)$ and the model with $(c^{2}\theta,\ c\,d_p,\ c^{2}\Sigma_p)$ generate the same distribution of $u$: the second is the first with the hidden state measured in different units. Their evidence $p(u)$ is identical for every $u$.

At $c = 2$, the evidence for Week 2's observation is $0.7215813$ under Week 2's parameters and $0.7215813$ under $(\theta, d_p, \Sigma_p) = (4, 4, 4)$, by quadrature in values.py.

Any rule that depends only on the evidence is therefore indifferent along the whole curve of rescaled parameters. What the observations can fix is the combinations that do not change along it, such as $\theta/d_p^{2}$ and $\Sigma_p/d_p^{2}$. The animal can learn how loud a typical source sounds and how much that varies. It cannot learn from sound alone how far away sources are in metres.

The rule of this week does not depend only on the evidence, and along this curve it is not indifferent.

DerivationThe settled log joint along the rescaling

The prior density of $d' = c\,d$ at $c\,d$ is the density of $d$ at $d$ divided by $c$, since $\mathrm dd' = c\,\mathrm dd$, and the likelihood is unchanged because the prediction is. So the log joint of the rescaled model at $c\,d$ is $\ell(d) - \ln c$. Maximising over $d$ is the same as maximising over $c\,d$, so

$$\hat\ell\big(u;\ c^{2}\theta,\ c\,d_p,\ c^{2}\Sigma_p\big) \;=\; \hat\ell\big(u;\ \theta, d_p, \Sigma_p\big) \;-\; \ln c .$$

Differentiate with respect to $c$ at $c = 1$. The chain rule gives the parameters' rates of change $2\theta$, $d_p$ and $2\Sigma_p$, so

$$2\theta\,\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\theta} \;+\; d_p\,\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial d_p} \;+\; 2\Sigma_p\,\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\Sigma_p} \;=\; -1$$
(3.3.4)

for every observation.

At Week 2's observation the left-hand side of (3.3.4), by finite differences, is $-1.000000$, and $\hat\ell$ falls from $-0.7999$ to $-1.4931$ at $c = 2$, a drop of $\ln 2$. Since (3.3.4) holds on every trial, it holds for the drifts too, so the three drifts can never vanish together: learning all three by this rule has no resting point. Every trial pushes the parameters towards smaller $c$, and $\hat\ell$ grows without bound as $c \to 0$.

CarefulIdentifiability

A parameter that the evidence does not determine is unidentifiable. Updates that follow the evidence do not fail visibly along such a direction: they wander along it, or settle wherever the start and the noise leave them, and the learned values look like answers. Updates that follow the settled log joint, as this week's do, are worse off: (3.3.4) gives them a steady push along the direction, towards a sharper and nearer prior and a quieter source. An agent whose distances have to mean something, because it must reach the source, needs a second signal that fixes the scale, such as the time it takes to get there. Each widget in this week learns one parameter while the others are fixed, which removes the direction by fiat.

Exercise 3.3.1A link linear in the state

Suppose instead that $g(d, \theta) = \theta d$. Derive $\partial\ell/\partial\theta$ and say which unit's activity plays the role of $h(d)$. Is the evidence still invariant under some rescaling of $(\theta, d_p, \Sigma_p)$?

Solution

The sensory term is $-(u - \theta d)^2/(2\Sigma_u)$, so $\partial\ell/\partial\theta = \varepsilon_u\,d$. Here $h(d) = d$, so the sending unit is the estimate unit itself and no separate link-shape unit is needed.

Under $d' = c\,d$ the prediction is $\theta d = (\theta/c)\,d'$, and the prior on $d'$ has location $c\,d_p$ and variance $c^2\Sigma_p$. So $(\theta, d_p, \Sigma_p)$ and $(\theta/c,\ c\,d_p,\ c^2\Sigma_p)$ give the same evidence. The invariant combinations are now $\theta d_p$ and $\theta^2\Sigma_p$. Any link of the form $\theta h(d)$ with $h$ a power of $d$ has such a symmetry; the power sets how $\theta$ must change.

Exercise 3.3.2Reading the averages

In the widget with learning off, the average sensory error is close to zero while the link is wrong by a factor of two. Explain, using (3.3.3), why the learning signal is nonetheless positive. Then make the world quieter than the model, $\theta^{*} = 0.5$, predict the sign of the learning signal, and check it and the two simple error averages in the notebook. Does every world with $\theta^{*} < \theta$ give the sign you predicted?

Solution

The learning signal weights each trial's sensory error by $h(\hat d) = 1/\hat d^{2}$. In a world louder than the model, the trials with the loudest sounds are inferred as the nearest sources, so they have the largest $h(\hat d)$. On those trials the prior pulls the estimate back towards $d_p$, the estimate is farther than the sound alone would say, and the prediction falls short of the sound: $\varepsilon_u > 0$. On distant trials the opposite happens with small weight. The simple average of $\varepsilon_u$ can cancel while the weighted one does not.

At $\theta^{*} = 0.5$ the same argument with the signs reversed suggests a negative learning signal, so that $\theta$ falls. It says nothing about the simple averages. With learning off, the notebook gives averages over the last $200$ trials of $0.127$ for the prior error, $-0.946$ for the sensory error and $-0.0481$ for the learning signal, and the drift at $\theta = 1$, averaged over the process by quadrature, is $-0.0865$. The learning signal has the predicted sign. The two errors have the same signs as when the model is exactly right, because both are measured from the mode and carry the offsets described above.

The sign is not guaranteed for every quieter world. The argument compares the world with the model, but the rule also has the bias of reading the mode, and in a world equal to the model that bias alone pushes $\theta$ up, by Lesson 4. At $\theta^{*} = 0.99$ the drift at $\theta = 1$ is $+0.0364$: the world is slightly quieter, and the power still rises. The mismatch decides the sign only when it is large enough to outweigh the bias.
