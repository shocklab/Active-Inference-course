Hidden state estimation

Precision and prediction errors

Interpreting the gradient, deriving the linear Gaussian case, and specifying a prediction-error circuit.

Week 2§440 min

The result of Lesson 3 was

$$\dot{d} \;=\; \nu\big(\varepsilon_p + g'(d)\,\varepsilon_u\big), \qquad \varepsilon_p = \frac{d_p - d}{\Sigma_p}, \qquad \varepsilon_u = \frac{u - g(d)}{\Sigma_u}.$$
(2.4.1)

Three moving parts: two errors and a factor multiplying one of them. This lesson takes them one at a time, then puts the pieces back together as a circuit.

1Precisions

Dividing by a variance appears twice, so give it a name.

Lesson 3 defined the precision of a Gaussian channel as the reciprocal of its variance. Give the two of them symbols:

$$\Pi_p \;=\; \frac{1}{\Sigma_p}, \qquad \Pi_u \;=\; \frac{1}{\Sigma_u}.$$
(2.4.2)

Their units are the inverse of a squared measurement, so $\Pi_u$ is in inverse squared intensity and $\Pi_p$ in inverse squared distance. They are therefore not comparable numbers, and adding them directly would be adding a length to a temperature. Where a sum of precisions does appear later in this lesson it is because the link has been made the identity, so that $u$ and $d$ carry the same units and the two precisions become the same kind of quantity. With a general $g$ the conversion costs a factor of $g'^2$, which the curvature exercise at the end works out.

Precision

Writing inverse variance as precision makes the multiplicative gain on an error signal explicit. Weeks 5 to 8 make that gain a physical quantity that the system can adjust.

Rewriting (2.4.1):

$$\dot{d} \;=\; \nu\Big(\Pi_p\,(d_p - d) \;+\; \Pi_u\,g'(d)\,\big(u - g(d)\big)\Big).$$
(2.4.3)

Now the structure is visible. Each term is a raw error multiplied by a gain. The gain says how seriously to take that error, and nothing else in the expression says anything about seriousness.

With the numbers of this week, $\Pi_p = 1.0$ and $\Pi_u = 10.0$: these numbers have different units and cannot directly rank the two sources. The factor $g'(d)$ converts the sensory contribution into a gradient with respect to distance.

2Limits of the precisions

Push each precision to an extreme and read off what the rule does. These limits describe stationary conditions, provided the solution remains in the domain. They do not give global convergence guarantees.

LimitWhat the rule doesReading
$\Pi_u \to 0$only the prior term survives; $d \to d_p$A useless ear. The animal ignores what it heard and rests on what it expected.
$\Pi_u \to \infty$the sensory term dominates; $d$ moves until $g(d) = u$A perfect ear. The animal believes the measurement exactly and the prior stops mattering.
$\Pi_p \to 0$only the sensory term survivesNo expectations at all. A flat prior, and the estimate is whatever the data alone say.
$\Pi_p \to \infty$$d$ is pinned at $d_p$A limiting prior concentrated at its location. This mathematical limit alone is not a diagnosis of a psychiatric condition.
To derive these limits, set the gradient to zero:

$$0=\Pi_p(d_p-d)+\Pi_u g'(d)[u-g(d)].$$

For $\Pi_p\to\infty$ with fixed $\Pi_u$, divide by $\Pi_p$:

$$d-d_p=\frac{\Pi_u}{\Pi_p}g'(d)[u-g(d)].$$

If the stationary solutions stay near the positive location $d_p$, the right-hand side tends to zero, so they approach $d_p$. At a finite precision the sensory term can still shift the solution. For $\Pi_u\to\infty$, divide instead by $\Pi_u$. An interior limit with $g'(d)\ne0$ must satisfy $g(d)=u$. Our positive observation and inverse-square map have such a solution. For a non-invertible map or an observation outside its range, this conclusion would need reconsidering. Taking either precision to zero removes its term from the stationary equation. A zero prior precision here means a limiting optimisation problem with a flat prior; that prior on the positive half-line is improper.

Multiplying both precisions by the same positive constant leaves these stationary points unchanged. It changes the speed of the continuous dynamics at fixed mobility $\nu$, and can change the stability of a discrete update at fixed step parameter $\eta$. The stationary estimate and the numerical method used to reach it must therefore be considered separately.

Between the corners, the estimate slides. With $\Sigma_u = 1.0$ the mode sits at $1.9357$, close to the prior location of $2.0$. With $\Sigma_u = 0.01$ it sits at $1.4261$, close to the $1.4142$ the data alone would give. At the $\Sigma_u = 0.1$ used throughout, $1.5500$, in between.

3Sensitivity to the hidden state

The factor $g'(d)$ appears on the sensory term and not on the prior term. It has two jobs, and they are the same job seen twice.

The units explain why the derivative is needed. The raw residual $u-g(d)$ has units of intensity, but $\varepsilon_u=\Pi_u[u-g(d)]$ has units of inverse intensity. Multiplying by $g'(d)$, whose units are intensity per distance, gives inverse distance. This matches $\varepsilon_p=\Pi_p(d_p-d)$. The mobility $\nu$, in squared distance per unit time, converts their sum to $\dot d$, in distance per unit time.

It also accounts for sensitivity. $g'(d)$ measures how much the predicted intensity would change if the estimate moved. Where it is large, a small change in the estimate produces a large change in prediction, so a sensory error is strong evidence about the estimate. Where it is small, the observation barely distinguishes nearby distances, and the term correctly contributes little.

For the inverse square, $g'(d) = -2/d^{3}$, which falls off fast. At $d = 1.55$ it is $-0.5370$; at $d = 2.0$ it is already only $-0.2500$. Far from the source, loudness says progressively less about distance, and the rule knows it without being told.

The idea

The sensory error is weighted twice over: once by $\Pi_u$, how reliable the channel is, and once by $g'$, how informative this particular measurement is here. The first is a property of the ear. The second is a property of where the estimate currently sits, so it changes as the estimate moves.

The sign matters too. $g' < 0$ everywhere, since intensity falls with distance, so a positive sensory error, louder than predicted, drives the estimate down. Had the link been $g(d) = d^2$, the same error would drive it up. The rule needs no separate knowledge of which way round the world works; the derivative carries it.

4The linear Gaussian comparison

For comparison, let $d$ be a signed coordinate on the whole real line, give it an untruncated Gaussian prior, and measure it directly: $g(d)=d$. The observation now has units of distance. This changes the observation model and domain. We choose it because the Gaussian calculation is analytic. Returning to a positive-distance domain would truncate the resulting posterior.

DerivationThe linear case gives a precision-weighted average

Set the gradient to zero in (2.4.3) with $g(d) = d$, $g'(d) = 1$:

$$\Pi_p (d_p - \hat d) + \Pi_u (u - \hat d) \;=\; 0 .$$

Expand and collect the terms in $\hat d$:

$$\Pi_p d_p + \Pi_u u \;=\; \hat d\,(\Pi_p + \Pi_u),$$

so

$$\hat d \;=\; \frac{\Pi_p\,d_p + \Pi_u\,u}{\Pi_p + \Pi_u} .$$
(2.4.4)

The estimate is the average of the prior location and the measurement, each weighted by its precision, and the weights sum to one because they have been divided by their total.

Setting the gradient to zero located the peak. It did not show that the whole posterior is Gaussian, and Week 1 promised that it is, with its parameters written down in two lines. Here are the two lines.

DerivationThe linear posterior is exactly Gaussian

Work with the exponent of the joint, dropping constants that do not contain $d$:

$$\ln P(d)P(u\mid d) \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\Pi_p (d - d_p)^2 \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2}\Pi_u (u - d)^2 \;+\; \text{const}.$$

Expand both squares and collect powers of $d$:

$$-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big[\Pi_p d^2 - 2\Pi_p d_p d + \Pi_p d_p^2 \;+\; \Pi_u d^2 - 2\Pi_u u\, d + \Pi_u u^2\Big]$$
$$=\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big[(\Pi_p + \Pi_u)\,d^2 \;-\; 2(\Pi_p d_p + \Pi_u u)\,d\Big] \;+\; \text{const},$$

the last bracket having swallowed $\Pi_p d_p^2 + \Pi_u u^2$, which carries no $d$.

Write $\Pi = \Pi_p + \Pi_u$ for the total and $m = (\Pi_p d_p + \Pi_u u)/\Pi$ for the weighted average of (2.4.4). Then $\Pi_p d_p + \Pi_u u = \Pi m$, and the bracket is $\Pi d^2 - 2\Pi m d = \Pi(d^2 - 2md)$. Complete the square by adding and subtracting $m^2$ inside it:

$$\Pi\big(d^2 - 2md\big) \;=\; \Pi\big[(d - m)^2 - m^2\big] \;=\; \Pi (d-m)^2 \;-\; \Pi m^2 ,$$

and $\Pi m^2$ has no $d$ either, so it joins the constant. What is left is

$$\ln P(d)P(u \mid d) \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Pi\,(d - m)^2 \;+\; \text{const}.$$
(2.4.5)

Exponentiate. The posterior is proportional to $\exp\!\big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\Pi(d-m)^2\big]$, which is the Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(d;\, m,\, 1/\Pi)$ once normalised. Reading its two parameters straight off (2.4.5): the mean is the precision-weighted average $m$, and the precision is $\Pi = \Pi_p + \Pi_u$. Precisions of independent sources add.

Being Gaussian, it has no skew and its mode and mean coincide. The inverse-square example has a non-quadratic exponent. Restricting a Gaussian to a half-line can also introduce skewness; the widget shows this boundary effect separately.

Conjugacy

This is what Week 1 called conjugacy: the posterior landed back in the prior's family, so the update changed two numbers and not the form. It is what lets the same update run over and over without the expression growing, and (2.4.5) is the whole of why it works here. For the inverse-square $g$, the exponent is not quadratic in $d$, so this Gaussian calculation no longer applies.

The same square also gives the evidence $P(u)$, which Week 1, Lesson 5 said would be evaluated by completing it. That needs the constants the derivation above dropped, so keep them this time.

DerivationThe evidence of the linear model

With both normalising factors written out, and $d$ ranging over the whole real line,

$$P(d)\,P(u\mid d) \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\Sigma_p\Sigma_u}} \exp\!\Big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\Pi_p (d - d_p)^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}\Pi_u (u - d)^2\Big].$$

The derivation above showed that the exponent equals $-\tfrac{1}{2}\Pi(d-m)^2$ plus the terms that carry no $d$, which were $-\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\Pi_p d_p^2 + \Pi_u u^2 - \Pi m^2\big)$. Evaluate that bracket. Since $\Pi m = \Pi_p d_p + \Pi_u u$,

$$\Pi\big(\Pi_p d_p^2 + \Pi_u u^2\big) - (\Pi m)^2 \;=\; \Pi_p\Pi_u d_p^2 + \Pi_p\Pi_u u^2 - 2\Pi_p\Pi_u d_p u \;=\; \Pi_p\Pi_u (u - d_p)^2 ,$$

the $\Pi_p^2 d_p^2$ and $\Pi_u^2 u^2$ terms cancelling. Dividing by $\Pi$, the bracket is $\Pi_p\Pi_u (u-d_p)^2/\Pi$, and

$$\frac{\Pi_p\Pi_u}{\Pi_p + \Pi_u} \;=\; \frac{1}{\Sigma_p\Sigma_u}\cdot\frac{\Sigma_p\Sigma_u}{\Sigma_u + \Sigma_p} \;=\; \frac{1}{\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u}.$$

So

$$P(d)\,P(u\mid d) \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\Sigma_p\Sigma_u}} \exp\!\Big[-\frac{(u - d_p)^2}{2(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)}\Big] \exp\!\Big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\Pi (d - m)^2\Big].$$

Only the last factor contains $d$. Its integral over the real line is the Gaussian integral $\int e^{-\Pi(d-m)^2/2}\,\mathrm{d}d = \sqrt{2\pi/\Pi}$, and $1/\Pi = \Sigma_p\Sigma_u/(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)$. Multiplying,

$$\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\Sigma_p\Sigma_u}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\,\Sigma_p\Sigma_u}{\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u}} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_u)}},$$

and therefore

$$P(u) \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} P(d)\,P(u\mid d)\,\mathrm{d}d \;=\; \mathcal{N}\big(u;\, d_p,\, \Sigma_p + \Sigma_u\big).$$
(2.4.6)

Before any observation, $u$ is the sum of $d$ and an independent noise, so its mean is $d_p$ and its variance the sum of the two variances. At the lesson's values the formula gives $P(u = 0.5) = 0.136788$; integrating the joint numerically over $-6 < d < 8$ gives $0.136788$.

Completing a square is an identity, so it can be checked rather than trusted. Divide the exact linear posterior by the claimed Gaussian on a fine grid: the quotient must be constant in $d$, not merely small. Over the whole grid that quotient, which is of order one, varies by $6.1 \times 10^{-14}$ between its largest and smallest values. That is floating point, not algebra.

One more check, and it needs no arithmetic. Set the two variances equal. Then $\Pi_p = \Pi_u$, the weights in $m$ are equal, and the estimate must be the plain average $(d_p + u)/2 = 1.25$, whatever the common variance happens to be. Equal confidence, equal say.

Put the numbers in. $\Pi_p = 1.0$, $\Pi_u = 10.0$, $d_p = 2.0$, $u = 0.5$:

$$\hat d \;=\; \frac{1.0 \times 2.0 + 10.0 \times 0.5}{1.0 + 10.0} \;=\; 0.636364 ,$$

with variance $1/(\Pi_p + \Pi_u) = 0.090909$.

Checking that against quadrature on the same wide grid: the mode comes out at $0.636361$, the mean at $0.636364$, and the skew at $1.3 \times 10^{-15}$, which is zero to machine precision. Mode and mean agree to five figures and part in the sixth. The algebra says they are equal exactly; the disagreement is the spacing of the grid the mode was read off, which the mean does not suffer because it is an integral over every point rather than the location of one.

For the inverse-square model, the stationary equation is nonlinear. Multiply $\Pi_p(d_p-d)-2\Pi_ud^{-3}(u-d^{-2})=0$ by $d^5$, valid on $d>0$, to obtain

$$\Pi_p(d_pd^5-d^6)-2\Pi_uud^2+2\Pi_u=0.$$

This polynomial has degree six. That does not prevent numerical root finding, and an optimisation method must still distinguish maxima from minima and check for multiple stationary points. Lesson 5 exhibits two modes for another choice of parameters.

The nonlinear example uses a positive-distance prior from the outset. The linear comparison here has support on the real line, including negative values. Its negative mass is 1.7% for the chosen parameters. If we restrict it to $d>0$, it becomes a truncated Gaussian with different moments. The widget uses that positive domain, which explains why its linear mode and mean need not coincide.

5The circuit

Now assemble (2.4.3) as a diagram, taking seriously the constraints of Lesson 1. Give each quantity a unit and each multiplication a connection.

Trace it. The estimate $d$ sends a prediction $g(d)$ down to the sensory error unit, which subtracts it from what the ear delivered. That unit's activity is the precision-weighted error $\varepsilon_u = \Pi_u(u - g(d))$, the weighting being part of what the unit computes rather than something applied in transit. On the way back up the connection multiplies by $g'(d)$, so what arrives is $g'(d)\,\varepsilon_u$. The prior branch works the same way: the error unit holds $\varepsilon_p = \Pi_p(d_p - d)$, and its connection back has weight one. The estimate changes at mobility $\nu$ times the sum of the incoming terms.

Where the precision lives

Nothing forces the precision to be computed at the error unit rather than applied on the connection; the product is the same either way. Putting it at the unit is the convention Weeks 5 to 8 follow, because there the precision becomes something the system adjusts, and a quantity that is adjusted needs somewhere to live. Read the arrow labels as naming what multiplies the error on its way, which is $g'(d)$ on the sensory branch and $1$ on the prior branch.

Check it against Lesson 1. Every arrow carries one number from one unit to one unit. No arrow requires a sum over states, and no unit reads a variable held somewhere it is not connected to. The rule satisfies local computation.

Definition 2.4.1Predictive coding

A scheme in which a system maintains estimates of hidden states, sends predictions from those estimates down towards the senses, computes the mismatch between prediction and input at each stage, and sends that mismatch back up to correct the estimates. What flows downward are predictions; what flows upward are prediction errors.

The literature usually says hidden causes here rather than hidden states. It means the same thing, and the choice of word is a matter of emphasis: "state" is what the world is in, "cause" is what the world did to the senses. Week 1's $s$, Week 2's $d$ and the literature's causes are one object under three names.

The circuit implements the gradient we derived. It presupposes a way to compute $g(d)$ and $g'(d)$, suitable error signals and known precisions. Its mathematical form alone is not evidence that cortex implements it.

The update retains a point estimate. Week 4 introduces distributions and asks how their uncertainty can be represented. In several dimensions a Gaussian requires a covariance matrix, so communication and storage requirements change. Week 5 examines that extension. Week 3 addresses learning model parameters rather than treating them as known.

Exercise 2.4.1Where the curvature comes from

Lesson 3 needed $\ell''$ to bound the step size. Differentiate (2.4.3) once more to get it, and evaluate the pieces at $\hat d = 1.5500$. You will need $g''(d) = 6/d^{4} = 1.0394$ there.

Solution

Differentiating $\ell'(d) = \Pi_p(d_p - d) + \Pi_u g'(d)(u - g(d))$ with respect to $d$, the first term gives $-\Pi_p$. The second needs the product rule on $g'(d)$ and $(u - g(d))$:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}d}\Big[g'(d)\big(u - g(d)\big)\Big] = g''(d)\big(u - g(d)\big) \;+\; g'(d)\cdot\big(-g'(d)\big).$$

So

$$\ell''(d) \;=\; -\Pi_p \;-\; \Pi_u\,g'(d)^2 \;+\; \Pi_u\,g''(d)\big(u - g(d)\big).$$

The first two terms are always negative and bend the function downwards. The third has the sign of the residual sensory error and can be either.

At $\hat d$: the first pair is $-1.0 - 2.8841 = -3.8841$, and the third is $\varepsilon_u\,g''(\hat d) = 0.8379 \times 1.0394 = 0.8709$. Together, $-3.0132$, which is the number Lesson 3 used.

The quantity $\Pi_u g'^2 = 2.8841$ is the sensory channel's precision expressed about the state rather than about the observation, converted by the same $g'$ that appears in the update, squared because a precision is a reciprocal squared quantity. Dropping the $g''$ term and keeping only $-\Pi_p - \Pi_u g'^2$ is the Gauss-Newton approximation, and Week 4 will meet it again as the curvature of a Laplace approximation.

Exercise 2.4.2Two ears

The animal grows a second ear with its own noise, so it receives two independent readings $u_1$ and $u_2$ of the same source, with precisions $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_2$. Write down the log joint and derive the update rule. What happens to the resting point if the two ears are equally precise?

Solution

Independence given the state means the joint likelihood factorises, $P(u_1, u_2 \mid d) = P(u_1 \mid d)P(u_2 \mid d)$, so the logarithm is a sum:

$$\ell = \ln P(d) + \ln P(u_1 \mid d) + \ln P(u_2 \mid d).$$

Each new term is the same shape as before, so differentiating gives one more sensory term of the same form:

$$\dot d = \nu\Big(\Pi_p(d_p - d) + g'(d)\big[\Pi_1(u_1 - g(d)) + \Pi_2(u_2 - g(d))\big]\Big).$$

The single $g'(d)$ factors out, because both ears are looking at the same hidden variable through the same link. Each channel contributes its own error at its own gain, and the estimate responds to the sum. This is the sense in which evidence accumulates: adding a channel adds a term, and never requires the existing terms to be recomputed.

With $\Pi_1 = \Pi_2 = \Pi$ the bracket becomes $\Pi\big[(u_1 + u_2) - 2g(d)\big] = 2\Pi\big[\bar u - g(d)\big]$ with $\bar u$ the mean of the two readings. Two equally precise ears are exactly one ear of twice the precision, reporting their average. The resting point is what a single ear would give on the averaged reading, and it is reached with a stiffer curvature, so by the step-size bound of Lesson 3 a smaller step.
