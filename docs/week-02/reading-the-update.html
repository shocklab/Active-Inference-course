Hidden state estimation

Reading the update

The rule from Lesson 3 has three moving parts. Each one has a job, and together they are a circuit that has been proposed as a model of cortex.

Week 2§440 min

The result of Lesson 3 was

$$\dot{d} \;=\; \eta\big(\varepsilon_p + g'(d)\,\varepsilon_u\big), \qquad \varepsilon_p = \frac{d_p - d}{\Sigma_p}, \qquad \varepsilon_u = \frac{u - g(d)}{\Sigma_u}.$$
(2.4.3)

Three moving parts: two errors and a factor multiplying one of them. This lesson takes them one at a time, then puts the pieces back together as a circuit.

1Precisions

Dividing by a variance appears twice, so give it a name.

Lesson 3 defined the precision of a Gaussian channel as the reciprocal of its variance. Give the two of them symbols:

$$\Pi_p \;=\; \frac{1}{\Sigma_p}, \qquad \Pi_u \;=\; \frac{1}{\Sigma_u}.$$
(2.4.4)

Their units are the inverse of a squared measurement, so $\Pi_u$ is in inverse squared intensity and $\Pi_p$ in inverse squared distance. They are therefore not comparable numbers, and adding them directly would be adding a length to a temperature. Where a sum of precisions does appear later in this lesson it is because the link has been made the identity, so that $u$ and $d$ carry the same units and the two precisions become the same kind of quantity. With a general $g$ the conversion costs a factor of $g'^2$, which the curvature exercise at the end works out.

Why not just say variance

Because every rule in the rest of the course multiplies by a precision rather than dividing by a variance, and the multiplicative form is the one that reads as a gain on a signal. Weeks 5 to 8 make that gain a physical quantity that the system can adjust.

Rewriting (2.4.3):

$$\dot{d} \;=\; \eta\Big(\Pi_p\,(d_p - d) \;+\; \Pi_u\,g'(d)\,\big(u - g(d)\big)\Big).$$
(2.4.5)

Now the structure is visible. Each term is a raw error multiplied by a gain. The gain says how seriously to take that error, and nothing else in the expression says anything about seriousness.

With the numbers of this week, $\Pi_p = 1.0$ and $\Pi_u = 10.0$: the animal treats its ear as ten times more reliable than its expectations, in their respective units.

2The four corners

Push each precision to an extreme and read off what the rule does. These are not edge cases to be handled; they are the whole behaviour of the rule, seen at its limits.

LimitWhat the rule doesReading
$\Pi_u \to 0$only the prior term survives; $d \to d_p$A useless ear. The animal ignores what it heard and rests on what it expected.
$\Pi_u \to \infty$the sensory term dominates; $d$ moves until $g(d) = u$A perfect ear. The animal believes the measurement exactly and the prior stops mattering.
$\Pi_p \to 0$only the sensory term survivesNo expectations at all. A flat prior, and the estimate is whatever the data alone say.
$\Pi_p \to \infty$$d$ is pinned at $d_p$Certainty. No evidence can move the estimate, which is what makes an infinitely precise prior a description of a delusion rather than of knowledge.
The last of those needs a word more than the others, because "dominates" does not by itself mean "pinned". The argument is that $\dot d$ has to stay finite: the animal's estimate is a physical quantity and cannot move infinitely fast. In $\Pi_p(d_p - d)$ the first factor is growing without bound, so the product stays finite only if the second goes to zero at the matching rate. The single configuration compatible with that is $d = d_p$ exactly, so the estimate is pinned there and no finite sensory term can shift it.

The other three limits need no such argument: in each, one term simply vanishes and you read off the survivor.

Only the ratio matters, not the individual values. Multiplying both precisions by the same constant multiplies the whole bracket in (2.4.5) by that constant, which rescales $\eta$ and moves the resting point not at all. The resting point is set by $\Pi_u/\Pi_p$, and the rate is set by their overall size: the same distinction the step-size bound of Lesson 3 ran into from the other direction.

Between the corners, the estimate slides. With $\Sigma_u = 1.0$ the mode sits at $1.9357$, close to the prior mean of $2.0$. With $\Sigma_u = 0.01$ it sits at $1.4261$, close to the $1.4142$ the data alone would give. At the $\Sigma_u = 0.1$ used throughout, $1.5500$, in between.

3What $g'$ is doing

The factor $g'(d)$ appears on the sensory term and not on the prior term. It has two jobs, and they are the same job seen twice.

It converts units. $\varepsilon_u$ is an error in intensity, and $\dot d$ is a rate of change of distance. Something has to carry intensity into distance, and $g'$ has units of intensity per distance, so $g'\varepsilon_u$ comes out in inverse distance, matching $\varepsilon_p$. Without it the equation would be adding quantities of different kinds, which is how you know the chain rule was not optional.

It gates by sensitivity. $g'(d)$ measures how much the predicted intensity would change if the estimate moved. Where it is large, a small change in the estimate produces a large change in prediction, so a sensory error is strong evidence about the estimate. Where it is small, the observation barely distinguishes nearby distances, and the term correctly contributes little.

For the inverse square, $g'(d) = -2/d^{3}$, which falls off fast. At $d = 1.55$ it is $-0.5370$; at $d = 2.0$ it is already only $-0.2500$. Far from the source, loudness says progressively less about distance, and the rule knows it without being told.

The idea

The sensory error is weighted twice over: once by $\Pi_u$, how reliable the channel is, and once by $g'$, how informative this particular measurement is here. The first is a property of the ear. The second is a property of where the estimate currently sits, so it changes as the estimate moves.

The sign matters too. $g' < 0$ everywhere, since intensity falls with distance, so a positive sensory error, louder than predicted, drives the estimate down. Had the link been $g(d) = d^2$, the same error would drive it up. The rule needs no separate knowledge of which way round the world works; the derivative carries it.

4The case where it is all exact

Replace the inverse square with the identity, $g(d) = d$, so that the animal measures distance directly with Gaussian noise. Then $g' = 1$ and the whole apparatus collapses to something recognisable.

DerivationThe linear case gives a precision-weighted average

Set the gradient to zero in (2.4.5) with $g(d) = d$, $g'(d) = 1$:

$$\Pi_p (d_p - \hat d) + \Pi_u (u - \hat d) \;=\; 0 .$$

Expand and collect the terms in $\hat d$:

$$\Pi_p d_p + \Pi_u u \;=\; \hat d\,(\Pi_p + \Pi_u),$$

so

$$\hat d \;=\; \frac{\Pi_p\,d_p + \Pi_u\,u}{\Pi_p + \Pi_u} .$$
(2.4.1)

The estimate is the average of the prior mean and the measurement, each weighted by its precision, and the weights sum to one because they have been divided by their total.

Setting the gradient to zero located the peak. It did not show that the whole posterior is Gaussian, and Week 1 promised that it is, with its parameters written down in two lines. Here are the two lines.

DerivationThe linear posterior is exactly Gaussian

Work with the exponent of the joint, dropping constants that do not contain $d$:

$$\ln P(d)P(u\mid d) \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\Pi_p (d - d_p)^2 \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2}\Pi_u (u - d)^2 \;+\; \text{const}.$$

Expand both squares and collect powers of $d$:

$$-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big[\Pi_p d^2 - 2\Pi_p d_p d + \Pi_p d_p^2 \;+\; \Pi_u d^2 - 2\Pi_u u\, d + \Pi_u u^2\Big]$$
$$=\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big[(\Pi_p + \Pi_u)\,d^2 \;-\; 2(\Pi_p d_p + \Pi_u u)\,d\Big] \;+\; \text{const},$$

the last bracket having swallowed $\Pi_p d_p^2 + \Pi_u u^2$, which carries no $d$.

Write $\Pi = \Pi_p + \Pi_u$ for the total and $m = (\Pi_p d_p + \Pi_u u)/\Pi$ for the weighted average of (2.4.1). Then $\Pi_p d_p + \Pi_u u = \Pi m$, and the bracket is $\Pi d^2 - 2\Pi m d = \Pi(d^2 - 2md)$. Complete the square by adding and subtracting $m^2$ inside it:

$$\Pi\big(d^2 - 2md\big) \;=\; \Pi\big[(d - m)^2 - m^2\big] \;=\; \Pi (d-m)^2 \;-\; \Pi m^2 ,$$

and $\Pi m^2$ has no $d$ either, so it joins the constant. What is left is

$$\ln P(d)P(u \mid d) \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Pi\,(d - m)^2 \;+\; \text{const}.$$
(2.4.2)

Exponentiate. The posterior is proportional to $\exp\!\big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\Pi(d-m)^2\big]$, which is the Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(d;\, m,\, 1/\Pi)$ once normalised. Reading its two parameters straight off (2.4.2): the mean is the precision-weighted average $m$, and the precision is $\Pi = \Pi_p + \Pi_u$. Precisions of independent sources add.

Being Gaussian, it has no skew and its mode and mean coincide. That is the opposite of what Lesson 2 found for the same problem with the inverse-square link, and the contrast is the point: swapping the link is the only change, so the link is what was responsible.

Conjugacy, named

This is what Week 1 called conjugacy: the posterior landed back in the prior's family, so the update changed two numbers and not the form. It is what lets the same update run over and over without the expression growing, and (2.4.2) is the whole of why it works here. Nothing survives the move to a nonlinear $g$, because then the exponent is not quadratic and there is no square to complete.

Completing a square is an identity, so it can be checked rather than trusted. Divide the exact linear posterior by the claimed Gaussian on a fine grid: the quotient must be constant in $d$, not merely small. Over the whole grid that quotient, which is of order one, varies by $6.1 \times 10^{-14}$ between its largest and smallest values. That is floating point, not algebra.

One more check, and it needs no arithmetic. Set the two variances equal. Then $\Pi_p = \Pi_u$, the weights in $m$ are equal, and the estimate must be the plain average $(d_p + u)/2 = 1.25$, whatever the common variance happens to be. Equal confidence, equal say.

Put the numbers in. $\Pi_p = 1.0$, $\Pi_u = 10.0$, $d_p = 2.0$, $u = 0.5$:

$$\hat d \;=\; \frac{1.0 \times 2.0 + 10.0 \times 0.5}{1.0 + 10.0} \;=\; 0.636364 ,$$

with variance $1/(\Pi_p + \Pi_u) = 0.090909$.

Checking that against quadrature on the same wide grid: the mode comes out at $0.636361$, the mean at $0.636364$, and the skew at $-8.8 \times 10^{-16}$, which is zero to machine precision. Mode and mean agree to five figures and part in the sixth. The algebra says they are equal exactly; the disagreement is the spacing of the grid the mode was read off, which the mean does not suffer because it is an integral over every point rather than the location of one.

The idea

Compare that with Lesson 2, where the mode and mean of the same problem differed by 35%. The ingredients were Gaussian in both cases and the only difference is the link function. So the entire gap is the nonlinearity, and none of it is the choice of prior.

That also explains why the answer had to be found by climbing rather than by formula. (2.4.1) is a closed form because setting a linear gradient to zero is a linear equation. With $g(d) = 1/d^{2}$, setting (2.4.5) to zero gives a polynomial in $d$ of degree five, and there is no general formula for its roots. Iteration is not laziness; it is what is left.

The bill for the Gaussian prior

Lesson 2 flagged that a Gaussian over a distance puts mass below zero. Here is the size of it. In the linear case the posterior is centred at $0.6364$ with a standard deviation of $0.3015$, so 1.7% of its mass sits at negative distances, which is small but not nothing. In the nonlinear case the same prior is harmless: as $d$ falls towards zero, $g(d)$ runs to infinity and the squared sensory error with it, so the likelihood suppresses the region entirely. The mass below $d = 0.5$ is about $1 \times 10^{-30}$. The inverse square cleans up after the prior.

5The circuit

Now assemble (2.4.5) as a diagram, taking seriously the constraints of Lesson 1. Give each quantity a unit and each multiplication a connection.

Trace it. The estimate $d$ sends a prediction $g(d)$ down to the sensory error unit, which subtracts it from what the ear delivered. That unit's activity is the precision-weighted error $\varepsilon_u = \Pi_u(u - g(d))$, the weighting being part of what the unit computes rather than something applied in transit. On the way back up the connection multiplies by $g'(d)$, so what arrives is $g'(d)\,\varepsilon_u$. The prior branch works the same way: the error unit holds $\varepsilon_p = \Pi_p(d_p - d)$, and its connection back has weight one. The estimate changes at a rate given by the sum of what arrives.

Where the precision lives

Nothing forces the precision to be computed at the error unit rather than applied on the connection; the product is the same either way. Putting it at the unit is the convention Weeks 5 to 8 follow, because there the precision becomes something the system adjusts, and a quantity that is adjusted needs somewhere to live. Read the arrow labels as naming what multiplies the error on its way, which is $g'(d)$ on the sensory branch and $1$ on the prior branch.

Check it against Lesson 1. Every arrow carries one number from one unit to one unit. No arrow requires a sum over states, and no unit reads a variable held somewhere it is not connected to. The rule satisfies local computation.

Definition 2.4.1Predictive coding

A scheme in which a system maintains estimates of hidden states, sends predictions from those estimates down towards the senses, computes the mismatch between prediction and input at each stage, and sends that mismatch back up to correct the estimates. What flows downward are predictions; what flows upward are prediction errors.

The literature usually says hidden causes here rather than hidden states. It means the same thing, and the choice of word is a matter of emphasis: "state" is what the world is in, "cause" is what the world did to the senses. Week 1's $s$, Week 2's $d$ and the literature's causes are one object under three names.

That is what has been derived, and it was not the goal. The goal was to maximise $\mathcal{F}$, and the only steps taken were the chain rule and the decision to climb a gradient. The circuit is what those steps look like when drawn.

CarefulWhat has not been shown

Deriving a circuit that could be implemented in tissue is not evidence that it is. The argument here establishes that predictive coding is a correct gradient-ascent scheme for a particular estimation problem, and nothing about whether cortex does it.

Two gaps. First, the update carries a point estimate and no uncertainty at all: this is the point-mass case flagged in Lesson 3, and Week 4 puts the uncertainty back. Second, the precisions and the function $g$ have been treated as known, when an animal is given neither. Week 3 takes that up.

CarefulA contradiction to hold on to

Lesson 1 ruled out carrying a whole distribution, and that was the justification for spending this entire week on a single point. Lesson 3's warning then promises that Week 4 brings a distribution $Q(d)$ back. Those two cannot both stand as written, and a reader who notices is right to be suspicious.

The resolution is in the exact wording of Lesson 1: what was ruled out was carrying an arbitrary distribution, one whose shape belongs to no family and which therefore needs a value stored at every point. Week 4's $Q$ is restricted to a family with a few parameters, typically a Gaussian, so carrying it means carrying a mean and a precision. That is two numbers where this week carried one, not infinitely many.

The bill for that restriction is real and Week 4 pays it explicitly: the true posterior of Lesson 2 is not Gaussian, so no Gaussian $Q$ can equal it, and the whole of variational inference is the study of what you lose. This week's point estimate is the extreme case of the same trade, a $Q$ with one parameter and no width at all.

A second question is fairer still, and has no comfortable answer yet. With many hidden states that depend on one another, the precision is no longer a number but a matrix, and its off-diagonal entries couple variables that may sit far apart in the network. Whether local computation survives that is exactly the question Week 5 opens with, and the honest position until then is that this week has only shown the single-variable case.

Exercise 2.4.1Where the curvature comes from

Lesson 3 needed $\mathcal{F}''$ to bound the step size. Differentiate (2.4.5) once more to get it, and evaluate the pieces at $\hat d = 1.5500$. You will need $g''(d) = 6/d^{4} = 1.0394$ there.

Solution

Differentiating $\mathcal{F}'(d) = \Pi_p(d_p - d) + \Pi_u g'(d)(u - g(d))$ with respect to $d$, the first term gives $-\Pi_p$. The second needs the product rule on $g'(d)$ and $(u - g(d))$:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}d}\Big[g'(d)\big(u - g(d)\big)\Big] = g''(d)\big(u - g(d)\big) \;+\; g'(d)\cdot\big(-g'(d)\big).$$

So

$$\mathcal{F}''(d) \;=\; -\Pi_p \;-\; \Pi_u\,g'(d)^2 \;+\; \Pi_u\,g''(d)\big(u - g(d)\big).$$

The first two terms are always negative and bend the function downwards. The third has the sign of the residual sensory error and can be either.

At $\hat d$: the first pair is $-1.0 - 2.8841 = -3.8841$, and the third is $\varepsilon_u\,g''(\hat d) = 0.8379 \times 1.0394 = 0.8709$. Together, $-3.0132$, which is the number Lesson 3 used.

The quantity $\Pi_u g'^2 = 2.8841$ is the sensory channel's precision expressed about the state rather than about the observation, converted by the same $g'$ that appears in the update, squared because a precision is a reciprocal squared quantity. Dropping the $g''$ term and keeping only $-\Pi_p - \Pi_u g'^2$ is the Gauss-Newton approximation, and Week 4 will meet it again as the curvature of a Laplace approximation.

Exercise 2.4.2Two ears

The animal grows a second ear with its own noise, so it receives two independent readings $u_1$ and $u_2$ of the same source, with precisions $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_2$. Write down the log joint and derive the update rule. What happens to the resting point if the two ears are equally precise?

Solution

Independence given the state means the joint likelihood factorises, $P(u_1, u_2 \mid d) = P(u_1 \mid d)P(u_2 \mid d)$, so the logarithm is a sum:

$$\mathcal{F} = \ln P(d) + \ln P(u_1 \mid d) + \ln P(u_2 \mid d).$$

Each new term is the same shape as before, so differentiating gives one more sensory term of the same form:

$$\dot d = \eta\Big(\Pi_p(d_p - d) + g'(d)\big[\Pi_1(u_1 - g(d)) + \Pi_2(u_2 - g(d))\big]\Big).$$

The single $g'(d)$ factors out, because both ears are looking at the same hidden variable through the same link. Each channel contributes its own error at its own gain, and the estimate responds to the sum. This is the sense in which evidence accumulates: adding a channel adds a term, and never requires the existing terms to be recomputed.

With $\Pi_1 = \Pi_2 = \Pi$ the bracket becomes $\Pi\big[(u_1 + u_2) - 2g(d)\big] = 2\Pi\big[\bar u - g(d)\big]$ with $\bar u$ the mean of the two readings. Two equally precise ears are exactly one ear of twice the precision, reporting their average. The resting point is what a single ear would give on the averaged reading, and it is reached with a stiffer curvature, so by the step-size bound of Lesson 3 a smaller step.
